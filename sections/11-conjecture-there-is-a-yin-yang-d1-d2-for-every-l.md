## 11 Conjecture: There is a Yin, Yang, *d₁*, *d₂* for every link

§[10.2](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#sec-yinyang-origin) watched one pair of links: the reverse bisector link revolving about the forward bisector link, its ends tracing the yin and yang curves, and the weights $`d_1,d_2`$ as the real-axis crossings of that pair. *We conjecture that the same picture exists at every link of the chain.* On the critical line which reverse link crosses forward link *k* is a function of *T* alone. Off the line write $`C_{\ell}(k;\sigma,T)`$, since the segment intersection may depend on σ. Once that partner is named, the yin and yang formulas, the weights, and the remainder split are the same constructions with that pair in place of the bisector pair. The seven subsections record, in order:

1.  first we need to know which main sum links cross which dual sum links (§[11.1](#sec-links-crossing));

2.  then we find the yin and yang curves for each crossing pair (§[11.2](#sec-yinyang-any));

3.  then we use those yin-yang formulas to find the weights *d₁* and *d₂* for each link (§[11.3](#sec-d1-any));

4.  then we use those weights to make the Dirichlet sums $`\Sigma_{1x}`$ and $`\Sigma_{2x}`$, whose joints sit at the crossings (§[11.4](#sec-sum-x));

5.  then we use the crossing points to rearrange the main sum into the two free-vector sums of the half-links (§[11.5](#sec-half-link-sums));

6.  then we use the crossing pairing to generalize the partial-summand formula for ζ to unequal cutoffs, whose two extreme cuts are example formulas built on the first link of either chain (§[11.6](#sec-cut-any-link));

7.  and finally we list what in this section rests on numerics and is left unproved (§[11.7](#sec-unproved)).

<a id="sec-links-crossing"></a>

### 11.1 Links crossing

§[9.1](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-bisector) named one crossing of the two link chains: the forward bisector link meets the reverse bisector link, and where they meet is the bisector point *B₁*. All the other links of the forward chain are crossed by some link of the reverse chain as well, and which one it is depends on *T*. Watching the link at $`m-1`$ as *T*, for example, runs from $`6`$ to $`7`$, one reverse link crosses it, and that link is joined by its neighbor that subsequently also crosses over it, and by the end of the unit interval the crossing links have advanced by two.

Numbering summands from one, so that link *k* carries the summand $`n=k+1`$ (§[7](07-summands-as-links-and-joints.md#sec-matrix-product)), forward link *k* and reverse link *j* cross when their two summands are reciprocal about the Riemann–Siegel cutoff $`a=\sqrt{t/2\pi}`$ of Appendix [A.2](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#sec-phase-bound), as nearly as two integers can be. Under $`t=I(T)`$ that cutoff is a function of the index alone, by [(91)](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#eq-IT-derived), so the rule closes in *T*:

<a id="eq-crossing-product"></a>

```math
n\,n'\;=\;a^2\;=\;\frac{t}{2\pi}\;=\;\frac{I(T)}{2\pi}
\;=\;\frac{2T+1}{2\log\!\bigl(1+\tfrac1T\bigr)},
\qquad n=k+1.\qquad\text{(106)}
```

Nothing is approximated in $`a^2`$: it is a closed form in *T* rather than an asymptotic. But it is not an integer, so $`n'=a^2/n`$ is the real partner, and the reverse link that actually crosses carries the nearest integer to it. The product of the two integers therefore misses $`a^2`$, by up to $`n/2`$; the rounding is unbiased, its signed mean $`-9\times10^{-4}`$ over the sample below, with a typical miss of a quarter of a unit. Write

<a id="eq-crossing-named"></a>

```math
j_0(k,T)\;=\;\Bigl[\frac{a^2}{k+1}\Bigr]-1
\;=\;\left[\frac{2T+1}{2(k+1)\log\!\bigl(1+\tfrac1T\bigr)}\right]-1,\qquad\text{(107)}
```

with $`[\,\cdot\,]`$ the nearest integer. Nearest-integer alone misses at two families of half-integers. One is the offset [(92)](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#eq-siegel-cutoff-index) already recorded: Siegel’s cutoff *a* sits half a link past *T*, so it steps near the half-integers of *T* while the chains themselves step at the integers. The other is the joints of the hyperbola $`nn'=a^2`$ itself. The *crossing link* is the rule that names the reverse link integer and removes those misses,

<a id="eq-crossing-rule"></a>

```math
C_{\ell}(k,T)\;=\;
\begin{cases}
m & \text{if }k=m,\\[2pt]
j_0(k,T) & \text{if reverse link }j_0\text{ crosses forward link }k,\\[2pt]
j_0(k,T)-1 & \text{otherwise,}
\end{cases}\qquad\text{(108)}
```

and the crossing summand is $`C_s(n,T)=C_{\ell}(n-1,T)+1`$. The first line is the self-pair: $`a^2/(m+1)`$ is near a half-integer when *T* is near an integer, so *j₀* names $`m\mp1`$ at the ends of the unit index, *T*, while the two bisector links cross at every height. The third line is the other family, the half-integers of the product $`a^2/n`$ itself: when $`\{a^2/n\}\approx\tfrac12`$ the geometric crossing has already moved to the lower neighbor. Replacing $`a^2`$ by $`T(T+1)`$ or $`(T+\tfrac12)^2`$ does not remove either family; the product is already the exact $`I(T)`$, and it only moves the joints. The observed pairing suggests the unproved involutivity conjecture

```math
C_s(C_s(n,T),T)=n.
```

At $`T=6.18`$, where $`a^2=44.5389`$, [(107)](#eq-crossing-named) reads $`44,21,14,10,8,6,5`$ for $`k=0,\dots,6`$, and all seven are real crossings (Figure [40](#fig-links-crossing)). The sum rule $`k+j\approx2T-1`$ that the links beside the bisector obey is this hyperbola’s tangent there, and holds only there.

Two remarks on *a*, since it is easy to read it as a link number. It is not one: it is a real height, the self-dual summand $`n=n'=a`$ the law pairs about, the one at which the forward chain hands over to the reflected one, and by [(92)](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#eq-siegel-cutoff-index) it sits half a link past the bisector link, at $`a^2=(T+\tfrac12)^2-\tfrac1{12}+O(T^{-2})`$. Nor is it a fixed number over a unit interval, as $`\lfloor T\rfloor`$ is: $`a^2`$ climbs from $`42.17`$ to $`56.17`$ as *T* runs from $`6`$ to $`7`$, and it is that climb that walks each link’s partner along inside the interval. Replacing $`a^2`$ by $`\lfloor T\rfloor^2=36`$ or $`(\lfloor T\rfloor+1)^2=49`$ at $`T=6.18`$ would name reverse link $`35`$ or $`48`$ as crossing the unit step, and neither does.

<a id="the-reverse-chain-is-the-forward-chain-reflected-in-the-bisector-line-"></a>

##### The reverse chain is the forward chain reflected in the bisector line.

Number the joints of both chains from zero as in §[7.2](07-summands-as-links-and-joints.md#sec-product-form),

<a id="eq-crossing-joints"></a>

```math
J_n=\sum_{n'\leq n}n'^{-s},
\qquad
K_j=\zeta-\chi(s)\sum_{n\leq j}n^{\,s-1},\qquad\text{(109)}
```

so that $`J_0=0`$, $`K_0=\zeta`$, and $`K_m=\Sigma_1+R`$ is the near end of the reverse bisector link; link *n* of either chain runs from its joint *n* to its joint $`n+1`$. On $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$ we have $`n^{\,s-1}=\overline{n^{-s}}`$, so $`K_j=\zeta-\chi\overline{J_j}`$, and with $`\chi=e^{-2i\vartheta}`$ and $`Z=e^{i\vartheta}\zeta`$ real (Hardy’s *Z*, as in [(200)](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#eq-r-two-cases)),

<a id="eq-chain-mirror"></a>

```math
e^{i\vartheta}K_j \;=\; Z-\overline{e^{i\vartheta}J_j}\,.\qquad\text{(110)}
```

Write $`e^{i\vartheta}J_n=X_n+iY_n`$. Then [(110)](#eq-chain-mirror) puts reverse joint *j* at $`(Z-X_j,\,Y_j)`$: in the ϑ-rotated frame the reverse chain is the forward chain reflected in the vertical line $`X=Z/2`$, joint for joint and link for link. That line is the bisector line of §[9.2](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-legs): the rotation puts ζ at *Z* on the real axis, so unrotated it is the perpendicular bisector of the base running from the origin to ζ, drawn dotted in Figures [16](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#fig-full-spirals) and [18](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#fig-legs) through $`\zeta/2`$ and the bisector point *B₁* (Corollary [9.2](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#cor-bisector-proj)). Doubling-back and the bisector line are two different things in the same neighborhood, and worth keeping apart: the place along the chain is the bisector link, where the chain doubles back; the bisector line is a line across the plane, the one the two chains reflect in. The bisector point is where the two meet, the point inside the bisector link at which the forward chain crosses the bisector line, as [(120)](#eq-crossing-rstar) works out below. This is the chain-level form of the reflection $`B_2=\chi\overline{B_1}`$ behind the equal legs of §[9.2](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-legs), and like it, it is a statement about the critical line only: off the line $`|\chi|\neq1`$ and the reverse chain is a scaled copy of the $`1-\sigma`$ chain instead of a reflection of this one.

<a id="crossings-come-in-pairs-"></a>

##### Crossings come in pairs.

Parameterize each chain continuously, the value $`\mu=k+p`$ with $`0\leq p<1`$ meaning the point *p* of the way along link *k*, and let $`X(\mu),Y(\mu)`$ be the rotated coordinates of the forward chain there and

<a id="eq-crossing-h"></a>

```math
h(\mu)\;=\;X(\mu)-Z/2\qquad\text{(111)}
```

its signed distance to the bisector line. By [(110)](#eq-chain-mirror) the reverse chain at ν sits at $`(Z-X(\nu),Y(\nu))`$, so the forward chain at μ meets the reverse chain at ν exactly when

<a id="eq-crossing-conditions"></a>

```math
h(\mu)+h(\nu)=0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
Y(\mu)=Y(\nu).\qquad\text{(112)}
```

Both conditions are symmetric in μ and ν, which is why crossings come in pairs: reverse link *j* crosses forward link *k* if and only if reverse link *k* crosses forward link *j*, and any rule naming the partner must be symmetric too, as [(106)](#eq-crossing-product) is. The pair coincides only when $`\mu=\nu`$, and then $`h=0`$; so a self-paired crossing lies on the bisector line itself, there is exactly one (the bisector point *B₁*, inside the bisector link of each chain by Theorem [A.7](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#thm-d1-bounds)), and every other crossing sits off the line with its counterpart on the other side.

<a id="sweeps-between-spiral-centers-"></a>

##### Sweeps between spiral centers.

Along either chain the turn from one link to the next is $`t\ln(1+1/n)\approx t/n`$, decreasing as the chain is walked, so the joints where that turn is an odd multiple of π lie near the spiral centers [(68)](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#eq-LN) (the midpoint of the link they bound is closer still), and between two consecutive centers the chain makes one full turn. The middle of a turn is the link where the angle is the even multiple $`2\pi c`$,

<a id="eq-crossing-sweep"></a>

```math
\frac{t}{n'}=2\pi c
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
n'=\frac{t}{2\pi c}=\frac{a^2}{c} ,\qquad\text{(113)}
```

and there successive links are parallel rather than antiparallel: their contributions add in phase, the chain stops circling and runs. Call that run the *c*-th *sweep*; it carries the chain from the spiral center $`S_n=c`$ to the spiral center $`S_n=c-1`$.

Heuristically, after inserting a smooth compact cutoff, Poisson summation separates the chain into oscillatory integrals. Stationary phase associates the *c*-th integral with the saddle $`x=a^2/c`$ and gives the displayed vector only as a leading asymptotic:

<a id="eq-crossing-saddle"></a>

```math
\frac{1}{\sqrt c}\,
\exp i\Bigl(\vartheta+t-t\ln\frac{a^2}{c}+\frac\pi4\Bigr)
\;=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt c}\,e^{\,i\left(2\Theta-(\vartheta-t\ln c)\right)},
\qquad
2\Theta=2\vartheta+t-t\ln a^2+\frac\pi4 .\qquad\text{(114)}
```

No exact Poisson identity is asserted for the unwindowed critical-line series. Its modulus is that of the *c*-th summand, $`c^{-1/2}`$, and its phase is the *c*-th summand’s phase $`\vartheta-t\ln c`$ reflected in the fixed direction Θ. Sweep by sweep, the tail of a chain reproduces the head of that chain reflected: this is the correspondence between joints and spiral centers recorded in §[9.1](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-bisector), Nickel’s center-to-center distances and angles reproducing the initial steps, now carrying an index; the *c*-th sweep answers to the *c*-th summand.

The reflection [(110)](#eq-chain-mirror) undoes the reflection in Θ. In the frame of the forward chain, therefore, the reverse chain’s *c*-th sweep is the forward chain’s own link $`c-1`$ traversed backwards: over the sampled heights the center-to-center step and that link agree to a median $`1.4\%`$, $`2.7\%`$, $`3.1\%`$ and $`5.2\%`$ of a link length for $`c=1,2,3,4`$. A copy of a link laid back along it must cross it, and the quarter turn in [(114)](#eq-crossing-saddle) is what tilts the copy so that it cuts across instead of lying flat: the two meet near the middle of the link, at some $`43^{\circ}`$. Which reverse link carries the *c*-th sweep is [(113)](#eq-crossing-sweep) with $`c=n`$, and that is [(106)](#eq-crossing-product). Panel (b) of Figure [40](#fig-links-crossing) is the case $`c=1`$: the whole outermost turn of the reverse chain, links $`30`$ to $`89`$, laid back along the unit step and crossing it at link $`44`$.

<a id="the-angle-at-a-crossing-"></a>

##### The angle at a crossing.

How the two links lie relative to one another needs no estimate at all. It is forced, and what forces it is that the two summands multiply to a whole number. In the rotated frame forward link *k* points along $`e^{\,i(\vartheta-t\log n)}`$ with $`n=k+1`$, and by [(110)](#eq-chain-mirror) reverse link *j* points along $`-e^{-i(\vartheta-t\log n')}`$ with $`n'=j+1`$, so the angle from the first to the second is

<a id="eq-crossing-angle"></a>

```math
\theta(n,n')\;=\;\pi-2\vartheta(t)+t\log(n n')\pmod{2\pi},\qquad\text{(115)}
```

exactly, whether or not the two links meet.

<a id="prop-crossing-angle"></a>


**Proposition 11.1**. *Fix $`C>0`$. On $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$, for positive integers $`n,n'`$ satisfying $`|nn'-a^2|\leq Ca`$, the displayed expansion holds with error $`O_C(1/a)`$:*

<a id="eq-crossing-angle-reduced"></a>

```math
\theta(n,n')
\;\equiv\;
\frac{5\pi}{4}\;-\;\frac{\pi\,(nn'-a^2)^2}{a^2}
\;+\;O_C\!\left(\frac1a\right)
\pmod{2\pi}.\qquad\text{(116)}
```

*In particular, if $`|nn'-a^2|\leq a/2`$ the two links are never parallel: they meet at an angle between $`\tfrac34\pi`$ and π, that is between $`135^{\circ}`$ and $`180^{\circ}`$, at most a quarter turn away from antiparallel.*


The proof depends on $`N=nn'`$ being an *integer*. Write $`N=a^2(1+\epsilon)`$. Since $`t=2\pi a^2`$,

<a id="eq-crossing-angle-integrality"></a>

```math
t\epsilon\;=\;\frac{t\,(N-a^2)}{a^2}\;=\;2\pi N-t\;\equiv\;-t\pmod{2\pi},\qquad\text{(117)}
```

so in $`t\log N=t\log a^2+t\epsilon-\tfrac12t\epsilon^2+\tfrac13t\epsilon^3-\cdots`$ the first-order term is $`-t`$ modulo $`2\pi`$, whatever *N* is. The Stirling expansion of the Riemann–Siegel theta function $`\vartheta(t)`$ (Appendix [A.2](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#sec-phase-bound)) is $`2\vartheta(t)=t\log a^2-t-\tfrac{\pi}{4}+\tfrac{1}{24t}+O(t^{-3})`$, so in [(115)](#eq-crossing-angle) the whole of $`t\log a^2-t`$ cancels against it and the $`\tfrac\pi4`$ survives:

```math
\theta\;\equiv\;\pi+\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{t\epsilon^2}{2}
+O\bigl(t|\epsilon|^3\bigr)+O\bigl(t^{-1}\bigr).
```

Here $`\tfrac12t\epsilon^2=\pi(N-a^2)^2/a^2`$. Under the proposition’s hypothesis, $`|\epsilon|\leq C/a`$, so $`t|\epsilon|^3=O_C(1/a)`$, which proves [(116)](#eq-crossing-angle-reduced). When $`|N-a^2|\leq a/2`$ we have $`|\epsilon|\leq1/2a`$, so the cubic term is at most $`\pi/12a`$ and the quadratic one at most $`\pi/4`$. Read as an unsigned angle, $`\tfrac54\pi`$ is $`\tfrac34\pi`$, and the correction can only carry it towards π.

The naming [(107)](#eq-crossing-named) stays inside that hypothesis: it makes $`|nn'-a^2|=n\,|n'-a^2/n|\leq n/2`$, and $`n\leq m<a`$ for every forward link below the bisector, so the $`135^{\circ}`$ is a floor and not an average: no link-crossing that the rule names can be sharper than that. Only the self-pair, being the bisector links, handled by the first line of [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule), where the rule overrides the rounding, can fall outside it. The quarter turn is the $`-\tfrac\pi8`$ of ϑ, which is the Gaussian factor of [(114)](#eq-crossing-saddle) over again: the $`43^{\circ}`$ by which a crossing misses antiparallel, the tilt that keeps the sweep from lying flat along the link it reproduces, is the stationary-phase quarter turn, read off exactly rather than measured. What the proposition fixes is the direction of the partner and not its position; that the two segments actually meet is verified numerically below but is not proved here.

<a id="near-the-bisector-a-sum-rule-"></a>

##### Near the bisector, a sum rule.

Near the self-dual link $`n=n'=a`$ the hyperbola [(106)](#eq-crossing-product) is indistinguishable from its tangent. Writing $`n=a+x`$,

<a id="eq-crossing-tangent"></a>

```math
n'=\frac{a^2}{n}=a-x+\frac{x^2}{a}-\cdots ,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
k+j\;=\;n+n'-2\;=\;2a-2+\frac{x^2}{a}-\cdots ,\qquad\text{(118)}
```

and since $`2a=2T+1-\tfrac1{12T}+O(T^{-2})`$ by [(92)](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#eq-siegel-cutoff-index), the sum is $`2T-1`$ up to the quadratic term and a drift of order $`1/12T`$: $`k+j`$ is one of the two integers straddling $`2T-1`$, that is

<a id="eq-crossing-sum"></a>

```math
j\;=\;\lfloor2T\rfloor-1-k
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
j\;=\;\lfloor2T\rfloor-k ,\qquad\text{(119)}
```

a rule needing nothing computed at all. The same rule comes out of the reflection [(110)](#eq-chain-mirror), which is worth doing because it identifies the fixed point. Near the bisector the forward chain sweeps across the bisector line and *h* passes through a single zero there, close to straight over the few links either side; writing $`r^{*}`$ for that zero and linearizing turns the first condition of [(112)](#eq-crossing-conditions) into $`\mu+\nu=2r^{*}`$, the two arcs straddling the crossing symmetrically, so $`k+j`$ straddles $`S=2r^{*}-1`$. And the crossing fraction is not a new quantity. By [(200)](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#eq-r-two-cases), $`h(m)=X_m-Z/2=-r/2`$ with $`r=e^{i\vartheta}R`$ the real remainder of Appendix [A.2](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#sec-phase-bound), and link *m* of the forward chain, being $`(m+1)^{-s}`$ rotated by $`e^{i\vartheta}`$, has horizontal extent $`X_{m+1}-X_m=\cos(\omega-\vartheta)/\sqrt{m+1}`$. So the zero falls inside link *m*, at the crossing fraction

<a id="eq-crossing-rstar"></a>

```math
r^{*}=m+\lambda,
\qquad
\lambda=\frac{Z/2-X_m}{X_{m+1}-X_m}
=\sqrt{m+1}\;\frac{r}{2\cos(\omega-\vartheta)}
=M^{1/2}d_1=\hat d_1,\qquad\text{(120)}
```

by the exact reduction [(199)](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#eq-exact-line) and the normalization [(24)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-frac-vs-weight). The place where the forward chain crosses the bisector line is therefore the bisector point *B₁* itself, sitting at the crossing fraction $`\hat d_1`$ along the bisector link exactly as Figure [31](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#fig-bisector-frame) marks it. So $`S=2r^{*}-1=2m+2\hat d_1-1`$, against $`2m+2\{T\}-1`$ from the tangent. These are not the same number: the limit of $`\hat d_1`$ is the waveform $`d(x)`$ of §[6](06-periodicity-in-t.md#sec-periodicity), not *x* itself, and the two part by nearly $`\tfrac12`$ at the ends of the unit interval. But the straddling pair depends only on which side of $`\tfrac12`$ each falls, and on that the two agree. The branch form [(31)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-d-profile) gives $`d(x)+d(1-x)=1`$, and on $`(0,\tfrac12)`$ the curve stays below $`\tfrac12`$: there $`d=\tfrac12+\mathcal W_{\infty}`$ with $`\mathcal W_{\infty}=-\tfrac12\tan(2\pi x)\tan\beta`$ [(25)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-W-infty), and the two tangents carry the same sign on each of $`(0,\tfrac14)`$ and $`(\tfrac14,\tfrac12)`$, both positive on the first and both negative on the second. So *d* crosses $`\tfrac12`$ exactly where *x* does, and the $`O(1/T)`$ of §[6](06-periodicity-in-t.md#sec-periodicity) can disturb this only within $`O(1/T)`$ of $`\{T\}=0,\tfrac12,1`$, which the sample below does not see it do. With $`\tfrac15<\hat d_1<\tfrac45`$ from Theorem [A.7](a-proof-of-proposition-4-1-reality-and-positivity.md#thm-d1-bounds) holding *S* within $`\tfrac35`$ of $`2m`$, the straddling integers are therefore $`\lfloor2T\rfloor-1`$ and $`\lfloor2T\rfloor`$ whichever side of $`\tfrac12`$ the fractional part is on, and [(119)](#eq-crossing-sum) follows either way: the tangent to the hyperbola and the linearization of *h* are the same line.

How far the tangent can be trusted is [(118)](#eq-crossing-tangent) again: it costs half a link once $`x^2/a=\tfrac12`$, so the sum rule is good only within

<a id="eq-crossing-reach"></a>

```math
|n-a|\;\lesssim\;\sqrt{a/2}\;\approx\;\sqrt{T/2}\qquad\text{(121)}
```

of the bisector (three links either side at $`T=17`$, fourteen at $`T=400`$, and just under two at the $`T=6.18`$ of the figure), which is why $`k=4,5,6`$ obey it there and $`k=3`$ already does not. Inside that range the two indices trade off one for one: each step back along the forward chain moves the partner one step forward along the reverse chain, and over one unit of *T* the sum moves by two, so each partner advances by two, in two single steps, one when $`\{T\}`$ passes $`\tfrac12`$ and one when *T* passes an integer, the two heights at which $`\lfloor 2T\rfloor`$ jumps. Outside the range the product takes over and the partners run away as $`a^2/n`$, reaching the far end of the chain at the unit step.

The bisector link is the one place where the pairing is exact rather than asymptotic, and that is the first line of [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule). It is the self-dual link, $`n=m+1`$ and $`x=\tfrac12-\{T\}`$, so [(107)](#eq-crossing-named) rounds to *m* only over the middle half of the unit interval and to $`m\mp1`$ at its ends; but the two bisector links still cross, and the self-paired crossing *B₁* of [(112)](#eq-crossing-conditions) is there at every height. The bisector links always cross, as §[9.1](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-bisector) has it, and the links *j₀* names near the ends of the interval are second crossings of the same forward link, not replacements for it.

##### How well it holds.

Sampling $`m\in\{8,17,40,123,400\}`$ at $`199`$ fractional parts each and testing every forward link $`0\leq k\leq m`$, $`118{,}007`$ cases in all, the link named by [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule) crosses forward link *k* in every one of them. The named integer [(107)](#eq-crossing-named) alone does so in $`117{,}870`$, $`99.9\%`$; the other $`137`$ are the two half-integer families of that naming, $`13`$ of them the self-pair (first line of [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule)) and $`124`$ the lower neighbor at $`\{a^2/n\}\approx\tfrac12`$ (third line), never the upper neighbor and never off by more than one link. The crossing sits at the middle of the forward link, median position $`0.500`$ along it with median deviation $`0.009`$ four or more links out from the bisector, widening to $`0.148`$ at the bisector link, where the crossing is *B₁* and the position is $`\hat d_1`$ instead. The two links meet at a median $`137^{\circ}`$, tenth to ninetieth percentile $`135^{\circ}`$ to $`151^{\circ}`$, and never below $`135^{\circ}`$ in any of them: that floor is Proposition [11.1](#prop-crossing-angle) and not a feature of the sample, and the exact form [(115)](#eq-crossing-angle) holds across the crossings to $`3.8\times10^{-9}`$, the accumulated double precision of the chains. The $`43^{\circ}`$ of the median is the quarter turn of [(114)](#eq-crossing-saddle) that keeps the sweep from lying flat along the link it reproduces. The center-to-center steps match those links to a median $`1.4\%`$, $`2.7\%`$, $`3.1\%`$ and $`5.2\%`$ of a link length at $`c=1,2,3,4`$, and the self-pair $`(m,m)`$ crossed at all $`995`$ sampled heights while [(107)](#eq-crossing-named) named *m* at exactly half of them, the middle half of each unit interval, as the previous paragraph says. The sum rule [(119)](#eq-crossing-sum), by contrast, names a real crossing in only $`10.6\%`$ of the cases overall, but in $`99.8\%`$ of those inside its range [(121)](#eq-crossing-reach) and $`5.1\%`$ of those outside it, which is what that range is worth. The identity $`\lambda=\hat d_1`$ of [(120)](#eq-crossing-rstar) holds to $`6\times10^{-15}`$. Computed in `check_links_crossing.py`.

### 11.2 General yin and yang form for any link

§[10.2](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#sec-yinyang-origin) watched one link cross another: the reverse bisector link revolving about the forward bisector link, its two ends tracing the yin and yang curves [(37)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-yin1)–[(38)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-yang1). Now that [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule) says which reverse link crosses forward link *k*, every link of the chain has such a pair, and they are all one formula. Like the bisector pair it carries no ζ: the remainder *R*, the factor χ, and σ and *T* are all it needs.

Send forward link *k* to $`[0,1]`$ exactly as [(36)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-frame-map) sends the bisector link: translate its joint $`J_k`$ to the origin and divide by the link vector $`(k+1)^{-s}`$, writing $`s=\sigma+iI(T)`$ throughout. Reverse joint *j* of [(109)](#eq-crossing-joints) lands at

<a id="eq-frame-joint-any"></a>

```math
Y_k(j)\;=\;\bigl(K_j-J_k\bigr)(k+1)^s
\;=\;(k+1)^s\left(\;\sum_{n=k+1}^mn^{-s}\;+\;R\;+\;\chi\!\!\sum_{n=j+1}^m\!\!n^{\,s-1}\right),\qquad\text{(122)}
```

the second sum read as $`-\sum_{n=m+1}^j`$ once $`j>m`$. What removes ζ is the same fact the bisector frame turned on: $`K_m=\Sigma_1+R`$, so measuring the reverse chain from its own joint *m* replaces the anchor ζ by the remainder. The yin and yang points of link *k* are then the two ends of the link that crosses it, which are consecutive reverse joints:

<a id="eq-yin-any"></a>

```math
Y_{in}(k,T)=Y_k(j),
\qquad
Y_{ang}(k,T)=Y_k(j+1)=Y_{in}(k,T)-\chi\,(k+1)^s(j+1)^{\,s-1},
\qquad j=C_{\ell}(k,T),\qquad\text{(123)}
```

with $`j=m`$ at the bisector by the first line of [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule).


**Remark 11.2**. Equation [(122)](#eq-frame-joint-any) is the general frame formula, and on the bisector it *is* the yin formula. It holds there because the crossing rule sets $`j=m`$ when $`k=m`$. Plug $`k=m`$ and $`j=m`$ into [(122)](#eq-frame-joint-any):

```math
Y_m(m)=(m+1)^s\left(\sum_{n=m+1}^mn^{-s}+R+\chi\sum_{n=m+1}^mn^{\,s-1}\right).
```

The first sum runs from $`n=m+1`$ to $`n=m`$ and is empty. The second sum runs over the same empty range (the joint is *m*, not past it, so the $`j>m`$ sign-flip is not used). What remains is

```math
Y_m(m)=(m+1)^s\bigl(R\bigr)=R\,(m+1)^s=\mathrm{Yin}(s),
```

which is [(37)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-yin1). Geometrically the same thing: $`J_m=\Sigma_1`$ and $`K_m=\Sigma_1+R`$, so $`(K_m-J_m)(m+1)^s=R\,(m+1)^s`$, and the frame map is [(36)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-frame-map) with $`M=m+1`$. The yang end is the next reverse joint $`j=m+1`$. Now $`j>m`$, so the second sum is $`-\sum_{n=m+1}^{m+1}=-(m+1)^{s-1}`$ and the first sum is still empty:

```math
Y_m(m+1)=(m+1)^s\bigl(R-\chi\,(m+1)^{s-1}\bigr)
=R\,(m+1)^s-\chi\,(m+1)^{2s-1},
```

which is [(38)](06-periodicity-in-t.md#eq-yang1), since $`2s-1=-1+2(\sigma+iI(T))`$. So [(122)](#eq-frame-joint-any) at the bisector is not an analogue of yin; it *is* yin. The closed-form loops of §[6.5](06-periodicity-in-t.md#sec-yinyang-general) and §[10.2](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#sec-yinyang-origin) are this formula with the two empty sums already canceled.


Take $`k=m-1`$. Its frame factor $`m^s`$ multiplies the summand $`m^{-s}`$, so that product is $`1`$ (forward joint *m* is the far end of that link), and

<a id="eq-frame-joint-below"></a>

```math
Y_{m-1}(j)\;=\;1+m^s\left(R-\chi\!\!\sum_{n=m+1}^j\!\!n^{\,s-1}\right).\qquad\text{(124)}
```

Here the crossing changes hands, which is the one thing the bisector’s does not do. By [(106)](#eq-crossing-product), $`a^2/m=m+2\{T\}+1+O(1/m)`$, so the crossing summand $`n'=j+1`$ runs $`m+1\to m+2\to m+3`$ across the unit and the curves come in three arcs:

<a id="eq-yin-below-a"></a>

<a id="eq-yin-below-b"></a>

<a id="eq-yin-below-c"></a>

```math
\begin{align}
n'=m+1:\quad
Y_{in}&=1+m^sR,
&Y_{ang}&=1+m^s\bigl(R-\chi(m{+}1)^{s-1}\bigr),
\qquad\text{(125)}\\
n'=m+2:\quad
Y_{in}&=1+m^s\bigl(R-\chi(m{+}1)^{s-1}\bigr),
&Y_{ang}&=Y_{in}-\chi\,m^s(m{+}2)^{s-1},
\qquad\text{(126)}\\
n'=m+3:\quad
Y_{in}&=1+m^s\Bigl(R-\chi\bigl((m{+}1)^{s-1}+(m{+}2)^{s-1}\bigr)\Bigr),
&Y_{ang}&=Y_{in}-\chi\,m^s(m{+}3)^{s-1},
\qquad\text{(127)}
\end{align}
```

the three running over $`\{T\}`$ in $`[0,0.214]`$, $`[0.215,0.647]`$ and $`[0.648,1)`$ at $`m=6`$ (Figure [41](#fig-yinyang-link5)).

Each arc’s yin is the preceding arc’s yang, the same point at the same *T*: both are $`Y_k(j+1)`$, once as the far end of the outgoing crossing link and once as the near end of the incoming one. That is the crossing of §[11.1](#sec-links-crossing) seen from the ends rather than the middle: one reverse link is dragged off the forward link and the next is dragged across it. The joint passing across the forward link belongs to both links at that instant, so the crossing itself goes through smoothly while the yin curve steps onto where the yang curve was and the yang steps one joint further out.

The count of reverse links dragged across forward link *k* in one unit of *T* is about $`2a/(k+1)`$: $`n'=a^2/(k+1)`$ advances by $`2a/(k+1)=(2T+1)/(k+1)+O(T^{-1})`$ over a unit of the index, and that is how many arcs the link gets. On the unit step, $`k+1=1`$, so about $`2a`$ reverse links cross it; at $`T=6.18`$, $`a\approx 6.67`$, so roughly a dozen. That is why the local coordinate frame for link $`0`$ in Figures [41](#fig-yinyang-link5)–[42](#fig-yinyang-extended) is a thicket of short arcs, so close together they are not visible separately in the figure, while the links beside the bisector only get two, and the bisector none. Its partner is the other bisector link at every height, so its yin and yang alone close into single loops, and they alone have the limit teardrop of §[6.4](06-periodicity-in-t.md#sec-yinyang-inf).

The two ends fix where the crossing reverse link sits along the forward link. In each separate local coordinate frame the link is $`[0,1]`$ on the real axis, so the crossing is where the chord from yin to yang crosses that axis, the computation [(99)](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#eq-crossing) makes at the bisector. Writing $`v_k=\chi\,(k+1)^s(j+1)^{\,s-1}`$ for the crossing link’s own vector in the frame, which by [(123)](#eq-yin-any) is the offset $`Y_{in}-Y_{ang}`$, that crossing fraction is

<a id="eq-crossing-fraction-any"></a>

```math
\lambda_k(T)
\;=\;\mathrm{Re}Y_{in}-\mathrm{Im}Y_{in}\,
\frac{\mathrm{Re}v_k}{\mathrm{Im}v_k}
\;=\;\frac{\mathrm{Im}\bigl(\overline{Y_{in}}\,v_k\bigr)}{\mathrm{Im}v_k}
\;=\;\bigl|Y_{in}\bigr|\,\frac{\sin(\beta-\alpha)}{\sin\beta},
\qquad
\alpha=\arg Y_{in},\quad \beta=\arg v_k,\qquad\text{(128)}
```

Let $`u_k`$ be the corresponding parameter on the reverse segment. The finite segments intersect exactly when $`0\leq\lambda_k\leq1`$ and $`0\leq u_k\leq1`$. The distance in the plane from joint *k* is $`\lambda_k\,(k+1)^{-\sigma}`$. At $`k=m`$ this is [(99)](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#eq-crossing) itself, $`\lambda_m=M^{\sigma}d_1`$, the bisector point’s own crossing fraction, which on the critical line is $`\hat d_1`$. So the rightmost panel of Figure [44](#fig-d1-any-link) is the quantity the rest of the paper is built on, and the panels to its left are that quantity’s counterparts link by link.

Checked against the local frame construction in the Zest web app on the critical line at $`T=6.18,\,6.72,\,12.4,\,17.4,\,40.05`$ and $`434.37`$: [(122)](#eq-frame-joint-any) matches the frame to $`1.3\times10^{-15}`$, [(128)](#eq-crossing-fraction-any) matches the app’s own reading to $`6.9\times10^{-14}`$, and $`\lambda_m`$ matches $`\hat d_1`$ to $`1.0\times10^{-12}`$, in `probe-yinyang-below-bisector.mjs`.

### 11.3 *d₁* and *d₂* for any link

§[4](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#sec-decomp) split Siegel’s remainder along the two bisector links, and §[10.2](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#sec-yinyang-origin) found the weights $`d_1,d_2`$ as the real-axis crossings of the yin and yang pair in those two frames. The same two crossings exist for every link: [(123)](#eq-yin-any) is the pair, [(128)](#eq-crossing-fraction-any) is the crossing fraction, and the weights are that fraction read once along the forward link and once along the reverse one.

Write $`n=k+1`$, $`n'=j+1=C_s(n,T)`$, and

<a id="eq-W-any"></a>

```math
W_k
\;=\;
\sum_{n=k+1}^mn^{-s}
\;+\;R
\;-\;\chi\sum_{n=m+1}^jn^{\,s-1}\qquad\text{(129)}
```

for the vector in the plane from forward joint *k* to reverse joint *j*, so that $`Y_{in}(k,T)=(k+1)^sW_k`$ by [(122)](#eq-frame-joint-any). At the bisector both sums are empty and $`W_m=R`$. Let

<a id="eq-omega-any"></a>

```math
\omega_n=I(T)\log n,
\qquad
\omega_{n'}=I(T)\log n',
\qquad
\varphi_k=\arg W_k,
\qquad
\psi=\arg\chi,\qquad\text{(130)}
```

so $`-\omega_n`$ is the absolute direction of summand *n* of Σ₁, the same telescoping that made $`\omega=\omega_{m+1}`$ in [(20)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#eq-omega-telescope), and $`\omega_{n'}+\psi`$ is the absolute direction of summand $`n'`$ of Σ₂.

<a id="measured-along-the-forward-link-"></a>

##### Measured along the forward link.

In the frame of link *k* the crossing fraction [(128)](#eq-crossing-fraction-any) is how far along that link it is from joint *k* to the crossing point. The frame scaled the link by $`(k+1)^{\sigma}`$, so the distance in the plane is

<a id="eq-d1-any"></a>

```math
d_1(k,T)
\;=\;
(k+1)^{-\sigma}\lambda_k(T)
\;=\;
|W_k|\,
\frac{\sin\!\bigl(\omega_{n'}-\varphi_k+\psi\bigr)}
     {\sin\!\bigl(\omega_n+\omega_{n'}+\psi\bigr)}.\qquad\text{(131)}
```

The sine form is [(128)](#eq-crossing-fraction-any) with $`Y_{in}=(k+1)^sW_k`$ and $`v_k=\chi\,(k+1)^s(j+1)^{s-1}`$, the same reduction that took [(99)](10-geometric-origins-of-the-i-t-and-yin-and-yang-fu.md#eq-crossing) to [(17)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#eq-d1): $`\alpha=\omega_n+\varphi_k`$, $`\beta=\omega_n+\omega_{n'}+\psi`$, and $`|Y_{in}|=(k+1)^{\sigma}|W_k|`$. At $`k=m`$ one has $`W_m=R`$, $`n=n'=\lceil T\rceil`$, $`\omega_n=\omega_{n'}=\omega`$, and [(131)](#eq-d1-any) is [(17)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#eq-d1).

<a id="measured-along-the-reverse-link-"></a>

##### Measured along the reverse link.

The same crossing, read along the reverse link from joint *j*, is the parameter $`u_k=\Im Y_{in}/\Im v_k`$ of the chord in that frame. The reverse link has length $`|\chi|\,(j+1)^{\sigma-1}`$, so

<a id="eq-d2-any"></a>

```math
d_2(k,T)
\;=\;
|\chi|\,(j+1)^{\sigma-1}\,u_k
\;=\;
|W_k|\,
\frac{\sin\!\bigl(\omega_n+\varphi_k\bigr)}
     {\sin\!\bigl(\omega_n+\omega_{n'}+\psi\bigr)}.\qquad\text{(132)}
```

$`d_1(k,T)`$ and $`d_2(k,T)`$ share a denominator, as *d₁* and *d₂* did. At $`k=m`$ this is [(18)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#eq-d2). The fractional amounts, the hat of [(24)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-frac-vs-weight), are the two parameters themselves:

<a id="eq-hat-any"></a>

```math
\hat d_1(k,T)=\lambda_k(T)=(k+1)^{\sigma}\,d_1(k,T),
\qquad
\hat d_2(k,T)=u_k=\frac{d_2(k,T)}{|\chi|\,(j+1)^{\sigma-1}}.\qquad\text{(133)}
```

<a id="the-direction-and-the-two-direction-form-"></a>

##### The direction, and the two-direction form.

Forward link *k* points along $`e^{-i\omega_n}`$ and reverse link *j* along $`e^{i(\omega_{n'}+\psi)}`$. The two fractional summands are those directions, shortened by the two weights,

<a id="eq-R-any"></a>

```math
R_1(k,T)=d_1(k,T)\,e^{-i\omega_n},
\qquad
R_2(k,T)=d_2(k,T)\,e^{\,i(\omega_{n'}+\psi)},\qquad\text{(134)}
```

and they add back to the vector they split:

<a id="eq-W-wedge"></a>

```math
W_k
\;=\;
d_1(k,T)\,e^{-i\omega_n}
\;+\;
d_2(k,T)\,e^{\,i(\omega_{n'}+\psi)}.\qquad\text{(135)}
```

This is the two-direction form [(21)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#eq-R-two-dirs) with *R* replaced by $`W_k`$ and the two bisector directions replaced by the two links that actually cross. The sine expressions [(131)](#eq-d1-any)–[(132)](#eq-d2-any) are again Cramer’s rule for that $`2\times2`$ real system, which is why they are real, and why they reduce to [(17)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#eq-d1)–[(18)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#eq-d2) at the bisector. On the critical line the bisector pair still satisfies $`d_1(m,T)=d_2(m,T)`$, but a general pair need not: the two links have different lengths, and the crossing sits at $`\hat d_1\approx\tfrac12`$ four or more links out from the bisector (§[11.1](#sec-links-crossing)), so $`d_1(k)`$ and $`d_2(k)`$ scale as $`n^{-\sigma}`$ and $`|\chi|n'^{\sigma-1}`$. Figure [43](#fig-Wk-triangle) is the same decomposition, one local frame per forward link, in the local frames of the top row of Figure [41](#fig-yinyang-link5): the last local frame is the bisector, $`W_6=R`$, which is Figure [9](07-summands-as-links-and-joints.md#fig-remainder-zoom) seen in that frame.

### 11.4 The crossings: $`\Sigma_{1x}`$ and $`\Sigma_{2x}`$ formula

§[11.1](#sec-links-crossing) named the reverse partner of each forward link, and §[11.3](#sec-d1-any) read the crossing fraction $`\hat d_1(k,T)`$ along that link. Those two data locate a point on every forward link $`0,\dots,m`$. Connecting the points in order is the same Dirichlet sum as $`\Sigma_1+R_1`$, re-jointed: the joints sit at the crossings instead of at the integers, and the walk ends at the bisector point *B₁* rather than at Σ₁. Call that walk $`\Sigma_{1x}`$.<sup id="fnref-11">[11](#fn-11)</sup>

The crossing fraction of link *k*, from joint *k* to the crossing point, is $`\hat d_1(k,T)`$ of [(133)](#eq-hat-any); *T* is fixed through what follows, so we write it $`\hat d_1(k)`$. Link *k* carries the summand $`n=k+1`$, so the crossing on it is

<a id="eq-sum-x-point"></a>

```math
P_k
\;=\;
J_k+\hat d_1(k)\,(k+1)^{-s}
\;=\;
\sum_{n'\le k}n'^{-s}+\hat d_1(k)\,(k+1)^{-s},\qquad\text{(136)}
```

with $`J_k`$ as in [(109)](#eq-crossing-joints). The last crossing is the bisector point itself, $`P_m=B_1`$. The walk $`0=P_{-1}\to P_0\to\cdots\to P_m`$ therefore telescopes to *B₁* by construction. Its first step is $`P_0=\hat d_1(0)`$, since $`J_0=0`$ and $`1^{-s}=1`$. Taking that step separately and substituting [(136)](#eq-sum-x-point) in the rest, with the empty sum $`\sum_{n'\le0}=0`$, removes the intermediate points and keeps $`0^{-s}`$ out of the display:

<a id="eq-sum-1x"></a>

```math
\Sigma_{1x}
\;=\;
\hat d_1(0)+\sum_{k=1}^m\Biggl(
\sum_{n'\le k}n'^{-s}+\hat d_1(k)\,(k+1)^{-s}
-\sum_{n'\le k-1}n'^{-s}-\hat d_1(k-1)\,k^{-s}
\Biggr)
\;=\;
\sum_{n'\le m}n'^{-s}+\hat d_1(m)\,(m+1)^{-s}.\qquad\text{(137)}
```

Each full summand $`k^{-s}`$ is divided across two consecutive steps, $`\hat d_1(k-1)\,k^{-s}`$ in one and $`(1-\hat d_1(k-1))\,k^{-s}`$ in the next, and those two pieces add to one. What remains is the integer partial sum plus the last fractional stub, which is the ordinary first-half formula [(47)](07-summands-as-links-and-joints.md#eq-B1-sum) once the stub is named.

That stub is $`\hat d_1(m)(m+1)^{-s}`$. The bisector link is link $`m=\lfloor T\rfloor`$. It runs from $`J_m=\Sigma_1`$ to $`J_{m+1}=\Sigma_1+(m+1)^{-s}`$, so the vector along it is the next Dirichlet term, $`n=M=m+1`$. The factor $`(m+1)^{-s}`$ is that whole unused summand, the faint dashed continuation past Σ₁ in Figure [8](07-summands-as-links-and-joints.md#fig-spiral-summands). The number $`\hat d_1(m)`$ is the crossing fraction: how far along the link the two bisector links meet. That meeting is *B₁*, and [(120)](#eq-crossing-rstar) already computed it: the crossing fraction on the bisector link is the remainder weight itself, $`\hat d_1(m)=\hat d_1`$. Therefore

<a id="eq-sum-1x-remainder"></a>

```math
\hat d_1(m)\,(m+1)^{-s}
\;=\;
\hat d_1\,(m+1)^{-s}
\;=\;
R_1,\qquad\text{(138)}
```

the “one more partial summand” of §[5](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#sec-summand): not a new term, the next summand scaled by a real signed coefficient. The unhatted weight *d₁* multiplies a unit vector in the direction of $`(m+1)^{-s}`$, so $`|R_1|=|d_1|`$. The hatted quantity $`\hat d_1`$ is a signed normalized coefficient, and is literally a fraction of the link when $`0\leq\hat d_1\leq1`$; it multiplies the full summand, whose length is $`M^{-\sigma}`$, and $`\hat d_1\cdot M^{-\sigma}=d_1`$ is the same remainder [(24)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-frac-vs-weight). At the running example $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$, $`T=6.18`$, one has $`m=6`$, next term $`7^{-s}`$ of length $`1/\sqrt7\approx0.378`$, $`d_1\approx0.0875`$, and $`\hat d_1=\sqrt7\,d_1\approx0.231`$: the walk uses about $`23\%`$ of link $`6`$, and that stub is *R₁*. The first six $`\hat d_1(k)`$ in the table of §[11.3](#sec-d1-any) sit near $`\tfrac12`$, as the saddle of §[11.1](#sec-links-crossing) said they would; only $`\hat d_1(m)`$ is the named remainder weight. So [(137)](#eq-sum-1x) is

<a id="eq-sum-1x-B1"></a>

```math
\Sigma_{1x}
\;=\;
\sum_{n'\le m}n'^{-s}+\hat d_1(m)\,(m+1)^{-s}
\;=\;
\Sigma_1+R_1
\;=\;
B_1,\qquad\text{(139)}
```

and not Σ₁ itself.

The same construction on the other Dirichlet sum gives $`\Sigma_{2x}`$. Write $`L_k=\chi(s)\sum_{n'\le k}n'^{\,s-1}`$ for the joints of Σ₂ from the origin, and $`\hat d_2(k)`$ for the crossing fraction of reverse link *k* from $`L_k`$ to its crossing point, the $`u_k`$ of [(133)](#eq-hat-any). Then

<a id="eq-sum-2x-point"></a>

```math
Q_k
\;=\;
L_k+\hat d_2(k)\,\chi(s)\,(k+1)^{s-1},\qquad\text{(140)}
```

and the same telescoping, with first step $`Q_0=\hat d_2(0)\,\chi(s)`$ and the empty sum $`L_0=0`$, is

<a id="eq-sum-2x"></a>

```math
\begin{aligned}
\Sigma_{2x}
&\;=\;
\hat d_2(0)\,\chi(s)+\sum_{k=1}^m\Biggl(
L_k+\hat d_2(k)\,\chi(s)\,(k+1)^{s-1}
-L_{k-1}-\hat d_2(k-1)\,\chi(s)\,k^{s-1}
\Biggr)\\
&\;=\;
\chi(s)\sum_{n'\le m}n'^{\,s-1}+\hat d_2(m)\,\chi(s)\,(m+1)^{s-1}.
\end{aligned}\qquad\text{(141)}
```

The last stub is $`\hat d_2(m)=\hat d_2`$, so

<a id="eq-sum-2x-B2"></a>

```math
\Sigma_{2x}
\;=\;
\Sigma_2+R_2
\;=\;
B_2.\qquad\text{(142)}
```

Nothing here used $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$. The split $`R=R_1+R_2`$ of §[4](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#sec-decomp) holds at every σ, the weights $`\hat d_1,\hat d_2`$ of [(24)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-frac-vs-weight) are defined at every σ, and so

<a id="eq-sum-x-zeta"></a>

```math
\Sigma_{1x}+\Sigma_{2x}
\;=\;
B_1+B_2
\;=\;
\zeta\qquad\text{(143)}
```

off the critical line as well as on it. On $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$ the two walks are the two legs of §[9.2](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-legs); off it they are just the two summands of Theorem [4.2](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1-r2.md#thm-decomp), re-jointed. Reflecting $`\Sigma_{1x}`$ through $`\zeta/2`$ sends the origin to ζ and fixes *B₁*, which is the other way of seeing the second half on the critical line, not the walk of $`\Sigma_{2x}`$ from the origin. The reverse partners of the forward links $`0,\dots,m`$ are not consecutive, so the points $`P_k`$ are not the joints of a walk along the reverse chain. This re-jointing is not Kapitonets’ iterated-midpoint polygon \[12\]: that construction is the binomial transform of the partial sums and does not aim at *B₁* or *B₂*.

### 11.5 First-part and second-part sums

Here we use the places where each link is crossed to divvy up the main sum into two separate sums: one adds up the first parts of all the summands, the other all the second parts. The remainder is handled automatically, because it is itself the first part of a summand. This subsection is nothing but an exploration of a rearrangement of the terms of the main sum.

Each forward link $`k=0,\dots,m-1`$ is cut at its crossing $`P_k`$ of [(136)](#eq-sum-x-point) into two pieces: a first part $`\hat d_1(k)\,(k+1)^{-s}`$ from the joint to the crossing, and a second part $`(1-\hat d_1(k))\,(k+1)^{-s}`$ from the crossing to the next joint. The bisector link contributes only its first part, the stub $`\hat d_1(m)\,(m+1)^{-s}=R_1`$ from Σ₁ to *B₁*. Summing those pieces as free vectors,

<a id="eq-half-link-sums"></a>

```math
V_1
\;=\;
\sum_{k=0}^m\hat d_1(k)\,(k+1)^{-s},
\qquad
V_2
\;=\;
\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\bigl(1-\hat d_1(k)\bigr)\,(k+1)^{-s},\qquad\text{(144)}
```

gives $`V_1+V_2=B_1`$: the full links $`0,\dots,m-1`$ plus the bisector stub are $`\Sigma_1+R_1`$. Figure [45](#fig-half-link-sums) draws *V₁* from the origin and *V₂* from the tip of *V₁*.

### 11.6 General partial summand Riemann zeta function

The partial-summand Riemann zeta formula [(23)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-zeta-clean), our main result, presented near the start of this paper, has the same summation upper bound in both the main sum and the dual sum. But with the knowledge of which links cross, we can make those two upper bounds different. This subsection presents that new, more general form of the Riemann zeta function.

For any nonparallel pair of summand directions, Cramer’s rule gives unique real coefficients and the displayed arbitrary-cut identity. The crossing conjecture is needed only to show that both coefficients lie in $`[0,1]`$. For $`j=C_{\ell}(k,T)`$, the corresponding coefficient relation, written with the joints of [(109)](#eq-crossing-joints), is

<a id="eq-cut-two-ways"></a>

```math
J_k+\hat d_1(k,T)\,(k+1)^{-s}
\;=\;
K_j-\hat d_2(k,T)\,\chi(s)\,(j+1)^{\,s-1},\qquad\text{(145)}
```

and moving the reverse sum inside $`K_j`$ across gives

<a id="eq-zeta-any-cut"></a>

```math
\begin{gathered}
\boxed{\;
\zeta(s)
\;=\;
\sum_{n=1}^kn^{-s}+\hat d_1(k,T)\,(k+1)^{-s}
\;+\;\chi(s)\Biggl[\;\sum_{n=1}^jn^{\,s-1}+\hat d_2(k,T)\,(j+1)^{\,s-1}\Biggr]
\;}\,,\\[4pt]
j=C_{\ell}(k,T).
\end{gathered}\qquad\text{(146)}
```

This is [(23)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-zeta-clean) with the two upper summation bounds unequal, though not independent: *j* is the crossing partner of *k* named by the rule [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule). At $`k=m`$ the pairing is the self-pair, $`j=m`$, both cutoffs are $`\lfloor T\rfloor`$, and [(146)](#eq-zeta-any-cut) is the boxed formula [(23)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-zeta-clean), hats and all. At any other *k* the two cutoffs differ: the forward sum stops at *k* while the reverse one runs to $`j\approx a^2/(k+1)`$, the two being reciprocal about *a* by [(106)](#eq-crossing-product). Reaching that far means carrying the reverse chain past its own bisector link, the continuation already written into [(129)](#eq-W-any).

For comparison, equation (4.13.1) of Titchmarsh’s book \[22, §4.13\], the approximate functional equation, states: if *h* is a positive constant and

```math
0<\sigma<1,\qquad 2\pi xy=t,\qquad x>h>0,\qquad y>h>0,
```

then

<a id="eq-titchmarsh-afe"></a>

```math
\zeta(s)\;=\;\sum_{n\le x}\frac{1}{n^s}
\;+\;\chi(s)\sum_{n\le y}\frac{1}{n^{1-s}}
\;+\;O\bigl(x^{-\sigma}\log|t|\bigr)
\;+\;O\bigl(|t|^{\frac12-\sigma}\,y^{\sigma-1}\bigr).\qquad\text{(147)}
```

The two sums are the same, and his constraint $`2\pi xy=t`$, that is $`xy=a^2`$, ties the two cutoffs exactly as the crossing product [(106)](#eq-crossing-product) does. Where he pays two error terms for the freedom to slide the cut, [(146)](#eq-zeta-any-cut) pays with the two fractional terms, exactly.

The extreme cases are the ends of the pairing. Take $`j=0`$ in [(146)](#eq-zeta-any-cut): the first reverse link is $`\chi(s)\,1^{\,s-1}=\chi(s)`$, so the reverse sum is empty and it is the second half that collapses to a single term,

<a id="eq-zeta-first-rev-link"></a>

```math
\boxed{\;
\zeta(s)
\;=\;
\sum_{n=1}^kn^{-s}+\hat d_1(k,T)\,(k+1)^{-s}+\hat d_2(k,T)\,\chi(s)
\;}\,,
\qquad k=\Bigl[\frac{I(T)}{2\pi}\Bigr]-1\ \text{ or one less},\qquad\text{(148)}
```

the forward cutoff being the partner of $`n'=1`$, about $`(T+\tfrac12)^2`$ links out. Which of the two it is the crossing test decides, exactly as in the third line of [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule): the nearest integer names the paired link at most heights and its lower neighbor at the rest, and off the critical line the choice can turn on σ as well. Here Σ₁ is walked whole, out to link *k* and a fraction of the next, and the walk lands on the segment hanging off ζ in the direction of $`-\chi`$. On the critical line that segment has length one, so the forward chain carried that far passes within one unit of ζ, and what is left over is $`\hat d_2(k,T)\,\chi(s)`$, a real multiple of χ. At $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$, $`T=6.18`$ the cutoff is $`k=44`$ and the two fractions are $`0.012402`$ and $`0.505537`$, reproducing ζ to $`9.9\times10^{-32}`$.

For comparison, equation (4.11.1) of Titchmarsh’s book \[22, §4.11\], the simplest approximation of $`\zeta(s)`$ in the critical strip by a partial sum of its Dirichlet series, states:

<a id="eq-titchmarsh-partial-sum"></a>

```math
\zeta(s)\;=\;\sum_{n\le x}\frac{1}{n^s}
\;-\;\frac{x^{1-s}}{1-s}
\;+\;O\bigl(x^{-\sigma}\bigr),\qquad\text{(149)}
```

uniformly for $`\sigma\ge\sigma_0>0`$, $`|t|<2\pi x/C`$, when *C* is a given constant greater than $`1`$. Both close one long partial sum with a small correction: his is the smooth tail $`-x^{1-s}/(1-s)`$ plus an error term, while [(148)](#eq-zeta-first-rev-link) closes exactly with the two fractional terms. And the cutoffs match in scale: his condition puts *x* at order $`t/2\pi`$ at least, which is where $`k=\bigl[I(T)/2\pi\bigr]-1`$ sits.

The cut at the other end of the pairing is the mirror of this one, $`k=0`$. The first forward link runs from $`J_0=0`$ to $`J_1=1^{-s}=1`$: it is the unit segment of the real axis. So the forward sum is empty, the link is the number $`1`$, and the entire first half of [(146)](#eq-zeta-any-cut) collapses to a single real number,

<a id="eq-zeta-first-link"></a>

```math
\boxed{\;
\zeta(s)
\;=\;
\hat d_1(0,T)
\;+\;\chi(s)\Biggl[\;\sum_{n=1}^jn^{\,s-1}+\hat d_2(0,T)\,(j+1)^{\,s-1}\Biggr]
\;}\,,
\qquad j=C_{\ell}(0,T).\qquad\text{(150)}
```

The reverse cutoff is the partner of the first summand, $`n'=a^2/1`$, so *j* is $`\bigl[\,I(T)/2\pi\,\bigr]-1`$ or one less, by [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule) again. On the critical line that is the same integer as the forward cutoff in [(148)](#eq-zeta-first-rev-link), the chain mirror [(110)](#eq-chain-mirror) carrying the one cut to the other; off the line the two part, as the measurements at the end of this subsection show. And $`\hat d_1(0,T)=d_1(0,T)`$ here: link $`0`$ has length one, so the fraction of the link and the distance along it are the same number, and this is the only link on which the hat of [(24)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-frac-vs-weight) does nothing.

Read as a walk, [(150)](#eq-zeta-first-link) says this. Start at ζ and come in along the reverse chain, *j* whole links and then a fraction $`\hat d_2(0,T)`$ of the next. The walk lands on the real axis, between $`0`$ and $`1`$, and $`\hat d_1(0,T)`$ is where. Equivalently, ζ minus that whole reverse tail is a real number in the unit interval. It is not just any point of that interval either: it is essentially the midpoint, the $`k=0`$ panel of Figure [44](#fig-d1-any-link) sitting on $`\tfrac12`$. The crossing fraction has the closed form of [(131)](#eq-d1-any) read at $`k=0`$, where $`\omega_n=I(T)\log1=0`$ and $`W_0=K_j`$ is the reverse joint itself:

<a id="eq-d1-first-link"></a>

```math
\hat d_1(0,T)
\;=\;
\mathrm{Re}K_j-\mathrm{Im}K_j\,\cot\bigl(\omega_{n'}+\psi\bigr),
\qquad n'=j+1,\qquad\text{(151)}
```

the crossing of the real axis by the line that carries reverse link *j*.

None of this is a shortcut to a value. The reverse sum now has about $`(T+\tfrac12)^2`$ terms where the balanced cut of [(23)](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#eq-zeta-clean) has $`2\lfloor T\rfloor`$ in all, and the two fractions are read off ζ just as *d₁* and *d₂* were: [(150)](#eq-zeta-first-link) is one complex equation in the two real unknowns $`\hat d_1(0,T)`$ and $`\hat d_2(0,T)`$, so it fixes them from ζ and not the other way about. What it fixes is a position. Nor is the crossing the only one: the continued reverse chain crosses the unit segment many times, $`87`$ times before link $`400`$ at the running example, since both chains wind. The pairing law picks one of them out, the link paired with the first summand, and [(151)](#eq-d1-first-link) says where that one lands.

At $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$, $`T=6.18`$, one has $`a^2=44.5389`$, so $`j=44`$, the tiny reverse link of the table in §[11.3](#sec-d1-any); then $`\hat d_1(0)=0.505537`$ and $`\hat d_2(0)=0.012402`$, and the right side of [(150)](#eq-zeta-first-link) reproduces ζ to $`4.6\times10^{-41}`$ at forty digits. The same cut at $`k=0,\dots,6`$ and that *T* gives seven formulas for the one number, with reverse cutoffs $`44,21,14,10,8,6,6`$: the list of §[11.1](#sec-links-crossing) with the self-pair in last place. Checked on $`500`$ samples of the critical line spread over $`2.05\le T\le60`$, and on $`100`$ more at $`\sigma=0.25,\,0.35,\,0.7,\,0.9`$: the crossing lay inside both links at every sample, and the two sides of [(150)](#eq-zeta-first-link) agreed to $`3.2\times10^{-30}`$ at thirty digits. The first crossing stayed near the middle of the unit segment throughout, $`\hat d_1(0,T)`$ in $`[0.5006,0.5233]`$ on the line and in $`[0.4858,0.5467]`$ off it, and on the line it approaches $`\tfrac12`$ from above as *T* grows: the product $`T\bigl(\hat d_1(0,T)-\tfrac12\bigr)`$ reads $`0.0354,\,0.0365,\,0.0370,\,0.0373`$ at $`T=10.5,\,20.5,\,40.5,\,80.5`$. The nearest-integer *j₀* was the paired link in $`481`$ of the $`500`$ line samples; at the other $`19`$ it was $`j_0-1`$, the third line of [(108)](#eq-crossing-rule), and at each of those the crossing sat at $`\hat d_2\ge0.98`$, at the far end of the reverse link and about to hand over. In `check_first_link.py`.

The two cuts are mirrors on the critical line: the fractions $`0.012402`$ and $`0.505537`$ of [(148)](#eq-zeta-first-rev-link) are the pair of [(150)](#eq-zeta-first-link) exchanged, because the chain mirror [(110)](#eq-chain-mirror) carries forward link *n* to reverse link *n* and so carries the one crossing to the other. Off the critical line the two formulas are independent, the mirror being what fails there: at $`\sigma=\tfrac14`$ and the same height the partner of reverse link $`0`$ is forward link $`43`$, with fractions $`0.9721`$ and $`0.5141`$, while the partner of forward link $`0`$ stays at reverse link $`44`$. The two cuts, which name one integer on the line, name two off it, and which of them drops turns over at $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$: at $`\sigma=\tfrac34`$ and $`\sigma=\tfrac9{10}`$ it is the reverse cutoff that reads $`43`$ and the forward one $`44`$.

<a id="sec-unproved"></a>

### 11.7 Left unproved

Section [11](#sec-general-yinyang) mixes identities that are proved, identities that are written down and then checked, and readings that are only measurements. Two statements about the crossings we would like to prove and have not.

1.  Every forward link is crossed by a reverse link, and the partner is the summand $`n'=a^2/n`$ of [(106)](#eq-crossing-product) (§[11.1](#sec-links-crossing)). The product law is motivated by a stationary-phase sweep and then checked on $`118{,}007`$ samples. Proposition [11.1](#prop-crossing-angle) settles the direction: the named partner is never parallel to the link and never more than a quarter turn from antiparallel, because $`nn'`$ is an integer. What is missing is the position. A theorem would have to show that the two polylines meet, not only that the named integer usually works, and that needs the sweep [(114)](#eq-crossing-saddle) with an effective error term rather than as a stationary-phase heuristic.

2.  The pairing is an involution: $`C_s(C_s(n,T),T)=n`$. Claimed as the symmetry of [(106)](#eq-crossing-product); if the previous item is proved, this is immediate.
