## 11 The yin and yang curves

As *T* increases the bisector point travels back and forth along the bisector link, and when *T* passes an integer it moves to the next link of the chain. It does so without any jump, as §[8.1](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-d1-exact) shows from the fold relation [(53)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-fold): the handoff happens exactly at the instant when the outgoing and incoming links fold back onto one another and momentarily occupy the same line in the plane (when $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$ the two bisector links are then coincident; for $`\sigma\ne\tfrac12`$ they are parallel). The path of the bisector point is therefore continuous both as a curve in the complex plane and as a position along the one-dimensional link structure.

§[10.1](10-i-t-functions.md#sec-IT-origin) watched the two links *neighboring* the bisector link in the bisector frame. Now watch the *reverse bisector link* in that same frame: holding the forward bisector link stationary while *T* increases, the reverse bisector link revolves around it, and each end of the revolving link follows a path reminiscent of a teardrop, the pair of paths together resembling a yin-yang (Figure [40](#fig-yinyang)). We call the paths of the two ends the *yin* and *yang* curves, and this section develops them: first their formulas, then the derivation of $`R_{1ps}`$ and $`R_{2ps}`$ from them, and finally their behavior as $`T\to\infty`$, where a familiar function makes an unexpected appearance. The same construction at every link is §[12](12-general-yin-and-yang-and-the-d1-and-d2-formulas.md#sec-general-yinyang).

The motivation is the question left open in §[9.1](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-bisector): the two bisector links cross, but *where*? The answer matters because, by [(77)](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#eq-bisector-point), the crossing (the bisector point) sits at distance *d₁* from the joint Σ₁ along the forward bisector link and at distance *d₂* from the joint $`\Sigma_1+R`$ along the reverse bisector link. If we can *compute* the crossing, we obtain *d₁* and *d₂*, and with them a new formula for ζ that uses the fractional summands in place of the usual remainders, all without evaluating ζ or either partial sum.

### 11.1 The formulas for the yin and yang curves

The bisector frame sends the forward bisector link to $`[0,1]`$, so the only question is where the *reverse* bisector link is. In the world frame its chain is anchored at ζ, which is both moving and precisely the quantity we do not wish to assume known; worse, the reverse bisector link also moves relative to its own anchor. The resolution is that the reverse chain’s joint *m* sits at $`\Sigma_1+R`$: relative to the forward joint Σ₁ it is displaced by exactly *R*, and *R* has its own integral representation [(16)](03-the-same-formula-reparameterized-the-i-t-mapping.md#eq-R-T), independent of any evaluation of ζ. Applying the frame map

<a id="eq-frame-map"></a>

```math
z \;\longmapsto\; (z-\Sigma_1)\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)}\qquad\text{(107)}
```

(translate joint *m* to the origin, then divide by the link vector $`\lceil T\rceil^{-\sigma-iI(T)}`$), the two ends of the reverse bisector link land at

<a id="eq-yin1"></a>

<a id="eq-yang1"></a>

```math
\begin{align}
Y_{in1}(\sigma,T) &\;=\; R\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)}, \\[2pt]
Y_{ang1}(\sigma,T) &\;=\; Y_{in1} \;-\; \chi\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,-1+2(\sigma+iI(T))},
\end{align}\qquad\text{(109)}
```

the first being the image of $`\Sigma_1+R`$ and the second the image of the far end $`\Sigma_1+R-\chi\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)-1}`$. As *T* sweeps, these two endpoints orbit the stationary unit link along the yin and yang curves of Figure [40](#fig-yinyang).

Because the frame map scales the bisector link to unit length, positions along it are *fractions*: the crossing we are about to compute is the fraction of the way along the link at which the bisector point sits, and multiplying that real fraction by the (unscaled) link vector recovers the fractional summand itself. The actual distance from the joint, the fraction times the true link length $`\lceil T\rceil^{-\sigma}`$, is the weight *d₁*.

The reverse side has its own frame and its own pair of curves. Sending the *reverse* bisector link to $`[0,1]`$ instead (translate its joint $`\Sigma_1+R`$ to the origin and divide by its link vector $`-\chi\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)-1}`$), the roles swap: now the forward bisector link revolves, its near end tracing

<a id="eq-yin2yang2"></a>

```math
Y_{in2}(\sigma,T)=\frac{R\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,1-\sigma}}{\chi\,e^{\,i\omega}},
\qquad
Y_{ang2}(\sigma,T)=Y_{in2}-\frac{\lceil T\rceil^{\,1-2\sigma}}{\chi\,e^{\,2i\omega}},\qquad\text{(110)}
```

with $`Y_{in2}`$ the image of the joint Σ₁ and $`Y_{ang2}`$ the image of the far end $`\Sigma_1+\lceil T\rceil^{-\sigma-iI(T)}`$, and with $`\omega=\omega(T)=I(T)\log\lceil T\rceil`$ as always.

The next two subsections derive $`R_{1ps}`$ from $`(Y_{in1},Y_{ang1})`$ and $`R_{2ps}`$ from $`(Y_{in2},Y_{ang2})`$. Each derivation is in two parts: first the modulus, found from the axis crossing; then the direction, which is simply that of the next summand.

<a id="sec-derive-r1ps"></a>

### 11.2 Derivation of $`R_{1ps}`$ from the yin and yang functions

<a id="first-the-modulus-"></a>

##### First, the modulus.

From its joint, how far along the forward bisector link do the two links intersect? This distance is $`|R_{1ps}|`$, because $`R_{1ps}`$ is the fractional part of summand $`\lceil T\rceil`$ of Σ₁, and that summand *is* the forward bisector link. In the local coordinate frame the link lies along the real axis, so the intersection is the point where the segment from $`Y_{in1}`$ to $`Y_{ang1}`$ crosses the axis. By the standard segment–axis intersection formula that crossing is

<a id="eq-crossing"></a>

```math
Y_{in1}
\;-\;
\frac{\Im\,Y_{in1}}
     {\Im\,Y_{ang1}-\Im\,Y_{in1}}\,
\bigl(Y_{ang1}-Y_{in1}\bigr),\qquad\text{(111)}
```

a real number. Substituting [(108)](#eq-yin1)–[(109)](#eq-yang1) and canceling $`Y_{ang1}-Y_{in1}=-\chi\lceil T\rceil^{\,-1+2(\sigma+iI(T))}`$ from the ratio,

<a id="eq-yang3lin"></a>

```math
\eqref{eq:crossing}
\;=\;
R\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)}
\;-\;
\frac{\Im\!\bigl(R\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)}\bigr)}
     {\Im\!\bigl(\chi\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,-1+2(\sigma+iI(T))}\bigr)}\,
\chi\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,-1+2(\sigma+iI(T))}.\qquad\text{(112)}
```

Writing $`\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)}=\lceil T\rceil^{\sigma}e^{i\omega}`$ and expanding the imaginary parts in components,

<a id="eq-compact5"></a>

```math
\eqref{eq:crossing}
\;=\;
R\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)}
\;-\;
\lceil T\rceil^{\,1-\sigma}\,
\frac{\Re R\,\sin\omega+\Im R\,\cos\omega}
     {\Re\chi\,\sin 2\omega+\Im\chi\,\cos 2\omega}\;
\chi\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,-1+2(\sigma+iI(T))},\qquad\text{(113)}
```

and finally, writing $`\varphi=\arg R`$ and $`\psi=\arg\chi`$ and collapsing with the sine difference identity $`\sin(2\omega+\psi)\cos(\omega+\varphi)-\cos(2\omega+\psi)\sin(\omega+\varphi)
=\sin(\omega-\varphi+\psi)`$, the whole expression reduces to

<a id="eq-frac1"></a>

```math
\eqref{eq:crossing}
\;=\;
\lceil T\rceil^{\sigma}\,|R|\,
\frac{\sin\!\bigl(\omega-\varphi+\psi\bigr)}{\sin\!\bigl(2\omega+\psi\bigr)}
\;=\; \lceil T\rceil^{\sigma}\, d_1 .\qquad\text{(114)}
```

The reader will recognize the sine quotient: it is, symbol for symbol, the definition [(19)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#eq-d1) of the weight *d₁*. *This is where that definition came from.* The crossing [(114)](#eq-frac1) is the crossing fraction (the “fractional amount” column of Table [1](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#tab-frac)) along the unit link at which the reverse link crosses, and dividing out the frame’s scaling $`\lceil T\rceil^{\sigma}`$ leaves the actual distance from the joint, *d₁*.

<a id="second-the-direction-"></a>

##### Second, the direction.

The forward bisector link points along the next summand of Σ₁: extending the summation cutoff by one,

<a id="eq-sigma1diff"></a>

```math
\sum_{n=1}^{m+1} n^{-\sigma-iI(T)}
\;-\;
\sum_{n=1}^m n^{-\sigma-iI(T)}
\;=\;
\lceil T\rceil^{\,-\sigma-iI(T)}\qquad\text{(115)}
```

(for non-integer *T*, so $`m+1=\lceil T\rceil`$). Multiplying the fraction [(114)](#eq-frac1) by this link vector undoes the frame map and yields the fractional summand itself:

<a id="eq-R1-derived"></a>

```math
R_{1ps}
\;=\;
\Bigl(\lceil T\rceil^{\sigma} d_1\Bigr)\cdot\lceil T\rceil^{\,-\sigma-iI(T)}
\;=\;
d_1\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,-iI(T)},\qquad\text{(116)}
```

which is definition [(17)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#eq-R1ps-def).

<a id="other-forms-"></a>

##### Other forms.

Two equivalent ways to write $`R_{1ps}`$ fall out of the same computation. Subtracting the crossing from $`Y_{in1}`$ instead of building it up from $`0`$ gives

<a id="eq-R1-other-a"></a>

```math
R_{1ps}
\;=\;
R
\;-\;
|R|\,\frac{\sin(\omega+\varphi)}{\sin(2\omega+\psi)}\;
e^{\,i(\omega+\psi)}\qquad\text{(117)}
```

(the subtracted term is exactly $`R_{2ps}`$, anticipating Theorem [4.1](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#thm-decomp)), and keeping the imaginary parts unexpanded gives the compact

<a id="eq-R1-other-b"></a>

```math
R_{1ps}
\;=\;
R
\;-\;
\chi\,e^{\,i\omega}\,
\frac{\Im\!\bigl(R\,e^{\,i\omega}\bigr)}
     {\Im\!\bigl(\chi\,e^{\,2i\omega}\bigr)} .\qquad\text{(118)}
```

<a id="sec-derive-r2ps"></a>

### 11.3 Derivation of $`R_{2ps}`$ from the yin and yang functions

The same procedure in the reverse frame, using $`(Y_{in2},Y_{ang2})`$ of [(110)](#eq-yin2yang2), locates the crossing of the (now revolving) forward bisector link with the real axis at the crossing fraction

<a id="eq-frac2"></a>

```math
\frac{\lceil T\rceil^{\,1-\sigma}}{|\chi|}\,
|R|\,\frac{\sin\!\bigl(\omega+\varphi\bigr)}{\sin\!\bigl(2\omega+\psi\bigr)}
\;=\;
\frac{\lceil T\rceil^{\,1-\sigma}}{|\chi|}\, d_2\qquad\text{(119)}
```

of the way along the unit reverse link, again the “fractional amount” column of Table [1](05-the-remainders-are-one-more-partial-summand.md#tab-frac), and again the sine quotient is, symbol for symbol, the definition [(20)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#eq-d2) of *d₂*: this is where *it* came from. (The two normalized fractions are nearly symmetric: since $`|\chi(\sigma+it)|=(t/2\pi)^{\frac12-\sigma}\bigl(1+O(1/t)\bigr)`$ and $`\sqrt{t/2\pi}\approx T+\tfrac12`$, the prefactor $`\lceil T\rceil^{\,1-\sigma}/|\chi|`$ is asymptotically $`\lceil T\rceil^{\sigma}`$, matching the forward side.) Multiplying by the reverse link vector $`\chi\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)-1}`$ undoes the frame map:

<a id="eq-R2-derived"></a>

```math
R_{2ps}
\;=\;
\frac{\lceil T\rceil^{\,1-\sigma}}{|\chi|}\,d_2
\cdot \chi\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,\sigma+iI(T)-1}
\;=\;
d_2\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,iI(T)}\,\frac{\chi}{|\chi|},\qquad\text{(120)}
```

which is definition [(18)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#eq-R2ps-def). As with the forward side there are equivalent rewritings, for example

<a id="eq-R2-other-a"></a>

```math
R_{2ps}
\;=\;
R \;-\; |R|\,e^{-i\omega}\,
\frac{\sin\!\bigl(\omega-\varphi+\arg\chi(1-\sigma+iI(T))\bigr)}
     {\sin\!\bigl(2\omega+\arg\chi(1-\sigma+iI(T))\bigr)}\qquad\text{(121)}
```

and

<a id="eq-R2-other-b"></a>

```math
R_{2ps}
\;=\;
R \;-\; e^{-i\omega}\,
\frac{\Im\!\Bigl(\dfrac{R}{\chi(1-\sigma+iI(T))\,e^{\,i\omega}}\Bigr)}
     {\Im\!\Bigl(\dfrac{1}{\chi(1-\sigma+iI(T))\,e^{\,2i\omega}}\Bigr)},\qquad\text{(122)}
```

where the reflected factor may be used interchangeably in the argument because $`\arg\chi(1-\sigma+iI(T))=\arg\chi(\sigma+iI(T))`$: from $`\chi(s)\chi(1-s)=1`$ and the Schwarz reflection $`\chi(\bar s)=\overline{\chi(s)}`$ one gets $`\chi(1-\sigma+it)=1/\overline{\chi(\sigma+it)}`$, which has the same argument as $`\chi(\sigma+it)`$ and reciprocal modulus.

<a id="the-poles-cancel-in-the-sum-"></a>

##### The poles cancel in the sum.

The two weights share the denominator $`\sin(2\omega+\psi)`$, and §[8.4](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-pole-locations) located the isolated heights at which it produces genuine poles. In the *sum* $`d_1+d_2`$ those poles cancel: adding the numerators with the sum-to-product identity $`\sin(\omega-\varphi+\psi)+\sin(\omega+\varphi)
=2\sin\bigl(\omega+\tfrac{\psi}{2}\bigr)\cos\bigl(\varphi-\tfrac{\psi}{2}\bigr)`$ and writing $`\sin(2\omega+\psi)=2\sin\bigl(\omega+\tfrac{\psi}{2}\bigr)
\cos\bigl(\omega+\tfrac{\psi}{2}\bigr)`$, the offending factor divides out:

<a id="eq-d1-plus-d2"></a>

```math
d_1+d_2
\;=\;
|R|\;
\frac{\cos\!\bigl(\varphi-\tfrac{\psi}{2}\bigr)}
     {\cos\!\bigl(\omega+\tfrac{\psi}{2}\bigr)} .\qquad\text{(123)}
```

The zeros of the remaining denominator $`\cos(\omega+\tfrac{\psi}{2})`$ are matched by zeros of the numerator (at those instants *R* is parallel to the common link direction, forcing $`\varphi\equiv-\omega\pmod\pi`$), so every singularity of [(123)](#eq-d1-plus-d2) is removable: the sum is smooth across all the poles of its two terms. Figure [41](#fig-d1-d2-sum) shows this at $`\sigma=0.1`$.

<a id="fig-d1-d2-sum"></a>

<p align="center"><img src="../figures/fig_d1_d2_sum.png"></p>

**Figure 41:** *d₁* (red), *d₂* (green) and their sum (blue) at $`\sigma=0.1`$, $`1\le T\le 7`$. Twice per unit of *T* the individual weights blow up at the parallel-link poles of §[8.4](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-pole-locations), but the poles are equal and opposite and the sum passes smoothly through every one of them; the black dashed curve is the closed form [(123)](#eq-d1-plus-d2), indistinguishable from the blue sum (they agree to machine precision on the plotted grid). Generated by `fig_d1_d2_sum.py`.

This is how the definitions of §[4](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#sec-decomp) were found: the sine expressions [(19)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#eq-d1)–[(20)](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#eq-d2) are nothing other than the real-axis crossings of the yang segments, read off in the two bisector frames. Only afterwards was Theorem [4.1](04-decomposing-the-remainder-r-r1ps-r2ps.md#thm-decomp) proved, confirming that the two fractional summands so constructed add back to Siegel’s *R* exactly. Note the economy of the construction: the bisector point, *d₁*, *d₂*, and both fractional summands are obtained from *R* and χ alone. Numerically we computed *R* to high accuracy by Kuznetsov’s method (§[7.1](07-other-remainders.md#sec-kuznetsov)), which is how the pictures, and then the identity, were checked.

### 11.4 Comparison of yin and yang to Siegel’s integral result

Siegel’s 1932 paper \[20\] evaluates the integral in his equation 5 to what he calls the “desired result”

<a id="eq-siegel-desired"></a>

```math
F(t)\;=\;
\frac{1}{1-e^{-2\pi i t}}
\;-\;
\frac{e^{\pi i t^2}}{e^{\pi i t}-e^{-\pi i t}} ,\qquad\text{(124)}
```

which can also be written

<a id="eq-siegel-desired-alt"></a>

```math
F(t)
=\frac{e^{2\pi i t}-e^{\pi i t(t+1)}}{e^{2\pi i t}-1}
=\Bigl(1-e^{\pi i (t^2-t)}\Bigr)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi i n t},\qquad\text{(125)}
```

the last form using the geometric series $`1/(1-e^{-2\pi i t})=\sum_{n\ge0}e^{-2\pi i n t}`$. Its complex conjugate has the compact form

<a id="eq-siegel-conj"></a>

```math
\overline{F(t)}
=\frac{e^{-\pi i t^2}-e^{-\pi i t}}{2\,i\,\sin(\pi t)} .\qquad\text{(126)}
```

<a id="rem-F-to-f"></a>


**Remark 11.1** (regarding *F* as the source of Siegel’s $`f(s)`$). *F* is not merely adjacent to the function *f* of [(46)](07-other-remainders.md#eq-siegel-58); it is what *f* is built from. In his §3 Siegel multiplies his equation 5, that is [(124)](#eq-siegel-desired), by $`u^{-s}`$ and integrates along the bisector of the first quadrant, and under that operation the two terms of *F* separate into the two halves of zeta. The pole term $`1/(1-e^{-2\pi iu})`$ yields $`-(2\pi)^{s-1}e^{\,i\pi(1-s)/2}\,\Gamma(1-s)\,\zeta(1-s)`$, while the Gaussian term yields *f* itself:

<a id="eq-f-from-F"></a>

```math
f(s)\;=\;\bigl(e^{\pi i s}-1\bigr)
\int_0^{e^{i\pi/4}\infty}
\frac{u^{-s}\,e^{\pi i u^2}}{e^{\pi i u}-e^{-\pi i u}}\,\mathrm{d}u
\qquad (\sigma<0),\qquad\text{(127)}
```

the restriction $`\sigma<0`$ being needed only for convergence at the endpoint $`u=0`$; *f* is continued analytically from there, and [(46)](07-other-remainders.md#eq-siegel-58) itself converges for every *s*. The same separation can be read off [(126)](#eq-siegel-conj): since $`1/(1-e^{2\pi it})=-e^{-\pi it}/(2i\sin\pi t)`$, the numerator term $`e^{-\pi it^2}`$ of $`\overline{F}`$ is the $`f(s)`$ side and $`-e^{-\pi it}`$ is the $`\zeta(1-s)`$ side, so the two lobes carry the two halves in their numerator (Figure [42](#fig-F-curve)). The yin-yang function is therefore the common source of both $`\Sigma_1+R_{1ak}`$ of [(45)](07-other-remainders.md#eq-siegel-f) and its reflected counterpart, which is one route into the convergence proof left open in §[11.6](#sec-yinyang-infinity).


<a id="fig-F-curve"></a>

<p align="center"><img src="../figures/fig_F_curve.png"></p>

**Figure 42:** The trace of $`\overline{F(t)}`$ of [(126)](#eq-siegel-conj) in the complex plane for $`-1.5\le t\le 1.5`$ (red dots every $`0.1`$ in *t*): the curve forms two congruent teardrop-like lobes, forming a shape reminiscent of the yin-yang symbol rooted in ancient texts like the *I Ching*. The curve is point-symmetric through $`\tfrac12`$. The apparent poles at integer *t* are removable, with $`\overline{F(t)}\to\tfrac12-n`$ as $`t\to n`$ (black squares). A curiosity: at *every* half-integer $`t=k+\tfrac12`$ the exponent $`t^2=k(k+1)+\tfrac14`$ has $`k(k+1)`$ even, so $`e^{-\pi i t^2}=e^{-i\pi/4}`$ always, and the whole expression collapses to just two values, $`\overline{F}(k+\tfrac12)=\tfrac12-(-1)^k\tfrac12 e^{i\pi/4}`$ (green diamonds): the curve returns to the point $`\tfrac12+\tfrac12 e^{i\pi/4}\approx0.854+0.354i`$ at $`t=\dots,-0.5,1.5,3.5,\dots`$ and to the point $`\tfrac12-\tfrac12 e^{i\pi/4}\approx0.146-0.354i`$ at $`t=\dots,-1.5,0.5,2.5,\dots`$, each revisited every $`\Delta t=2`$, forever; they are the meeting points of the lobes. Generated by `fig_F_curve.py`.

We conjecture that as $`T\to\infty`$ the yin and yang curves converge to a universal limit curve built from this function, universal in that it does not depend on σ. The precise limit is most cleanly written through the Ψ function, which we do next.

### 11.5 The yin and yang curves are not symmetrical

While the name “yin-yang” invokes a pictorial similarity as intended, it also invites a symmetry that is not there. In the taijitu the two lobes are congruent, each carried onto the other by a half-turn about the center. In our case, the two curves are the two ends of one moving link, and [(109)](#eq-yang1) says exactly how they are related:

<a id="eq-yang-offset"></a>

```math
Y_{ang1}\;=\;Y_{in1}\;-\;\chi\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,2s-1},
\qquad s=\sigma+iI(T),\qquad\text{(128)}
```

so the yang curve is the yin curve translated by $`-v`$, where $`v=\chi\lceil T\rceil^{\,2s-1}`$ is the revolving link’s own vector in the frame, of length $`|\chi|\,\lceil T\rceil^{\,2\sigma-1}`$. Were *v* the same at every *T* the two curves would be congruent by construction. It is not. Over one unit of the index the direction of *v* swings through π and comes back, returning to where it began, with net turning zero. The length of *v* varies too, and it is exactly $`1`$ only on the critical line, where $`|\chi|=1`$ and the exponent $`2\sigma-1`$ vanishes: the revolving link and the stationary link are then equal in length, which is the $`d_1=d_2`$ symmetry of §[9.2](09-the-geometry-behind-the-result-experimental-math.md#sec-legs) seen in this frame.

What the taijitu has is a half-turn about the center of the picture, and here that center is the midpoint of the stationary link: the half-turn is $`z\mapsto1-z`$, which exchanges the link’s two ends. So if the yin and yang curves were related as the taijitu’s two lobes are, this map would carry one onto the other, and it is enough to draw $`1-Y_{ang1}`$ on top of $`Y_{in1}`$ and look. Figure [43](#fig-yinyang-asym) plots $`Y_{in1}`$ against $`1-Y_{ang1}`$ for $`0\le T\le10`$, one loop of each per unit of the index, on the critical line and at $`\sigma=0.05`$ and $`\sigma=0.95`$ above and below it. The reflection brings the two families almost on top of one another, and both converge to the single limit teardrop of §[11.6](#sec-yinyang-infinity), but at no finite *T* do they coincide. Each loop crosses its reflected partner twice in every unit of the index, running outside it along one arc and inside along the other, and the two enclose different areas. That difference in area narrows as *T* grows and widens away from the critical line, where the two lobes also trade which of them is the larger, near the line though not exactly on it. In the magnified windows of Figure [43](#fig-yinyang-asym) the yin curve (green) is the one running outside its reflected partner (red) in the top two rows, and the inside one in the bottom row, where the order has swapped.

What the critical line does give exactly is a mirror symmetry, though between the two frames rather than between the two curves of one frame. At $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$ we have $`\lceil T\rceil^{\,s}=\lceil T\rceil^{1/2}e^{i\omega}`$ and, from $`\zeta=\chi\overline\zeta`$ and $`\Sigma_2=\chi\overline{\Sigma_1}`$ with $`|\chi|=1`$, also $`R=\chi\overline R`$. Comparing [(108)](#eq-yin1) with [(110)](#eq-yin2yang2) then gives

<a id="eq-frame-mirror"></a>

```math
\overline{Y_{in1}}=Y_{in2},
\qquad
\overline{Y_{ang1}}=Y_{ang2}
\qquad\bigl(\sigma=\tfrac12\bigr),\qquad\text{(129)}
```

which we confirm to $`25`$ digits. Off the line both identities fail, the first by $`6.4\times10^{-2}`$ and the second by $`7.1\times10^{-2}`$ at $`\sigma=\tfrac14`$, and by the same two amounts at $`\sigma=\tfrac34`$.

An exact symmetry between the two curves themselves does arrive, but only in the limit, where the yang curve stops being a second curve at all: as $`T\to\infty`$ the yang settles onto the very teardrop the yin traces, half a unit of the index behind. So the asymmetry of this subsection is a finite-*T* effect, and the limit erases it. That limit curve, and the classical function it turns out to be built from, are the subject of the next subsection.

### 11.6 The limit curve and the Ψ function: the *C₀* connection

As $`T\to\infty`$ with the fractional part $`q=T-\lfloor T\rfloor`$ held fixed, the yin curve converges, regardless of σ, to

<a id="eq-yinf"></a>

```math
Y_{\inf}(q)
\;=\;
1-\Psi(q)\,e^{-2\pi i\,(q^2-\frac{1}{16})},\qquad\text{(130)}
```

where

<a id="eq-psi-fn"></a>

```math
\Psi(x)\;=\;
\frac{\cos\!\bigl(2\pi(x^2-x-\tfrac{1}{16})\bigr)}{\cos(2\pi x)}\qquad\text{(131)}
```

is the Riemann–Siegel correction function (it appears in §15.3 of Titchmarsh’s book \[22\] and in §5.4.1 of Pugh’s thesis \[19\]). The yang curve follows the *same* universal function half a unit behind in the argument, $`Y_{ang1}(\sigma,T)\to Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)`$, but not the same teardrop: $`Y_{\inf}`$ is not $`1`$-periodic, and over one unit interval the yang end traces the point reflection of the yin teardrop through $`\tfrac12`$, the two congruent teardrops together forming the yin-yang of Figure [40](#fig-yinyang) (Lemma [11.2](#lem-psi-reflection) and Theorem [11.3](#thm-yinf) below make both statements exact). The measured convergence is uniform in *q* away from the removable points below, at rate $`O(1/T)`$, and essentially independent of σ: the maximum of $`|Y_{in1}-Y_{\inf}(q)|`$ over a *q*-grid is $`0.028,\,0.015,\,0.027`$ at $`T\approx10`$ and $`0.0076,\,0.0042,\,0.0075`$ at $`T\approx40`$ for $`\sigma=0.2,\,0.5,\,0.9`$ respectively. Figure [44](#fig-yinyang-infinity) shows the convergence, including the pleasing fact that the very first yin path is the zeta function itself: for $`0<T<1`$ both partial sums are empty and $`\lceil T\rceil=1`$, so $`Y_{in1}=R=\zeta(\sigma+iI(T))`$. The iconic zeta trajectory is yin orbit number zero.

The convergence is not merely an observation; it is a theorem, and the positivity machinery of §[8.5](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-d1-positive) already contains most of its proof: by [(108)](#eq-yin1) the yin point is *R* carried through the frame map, and §[8.5](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-d1-positive) knows the size, the sign, and the phase of *R* to exactly the accuracy needed. The one genuinely new ingredient is a reflection identity for the amplitude Ψ.

<a id="lem-psi-reflection"></a>


**Lemma 11.2**. *Write $`A(x)=2\pi\bigl(x^2-\tfrac1{16}\bigr)`$ and $`\theta(x)=2\pi x(1-x)-\tfrac{3\pi}8`$, the phase already met in [(70)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-cosu). Then for all real x, with removable points filled in by continuity,*

<a id="eq-psi-reflection"></a>

```math
1-\Psi(x)\,e^{-iA(x)}\;=\;\Psi\bigl(x+\tfrac12\bigr)\,e^{\,i\theta(x)},\qquad\text{(132)}
```

*and consequently the limit curve satisfies the exact half-lag relation*

<a id="eq-yinf-halflag"></a>

```math
Y_{\inf}(q)-Y_{\inf}\bigl(q-\tfrac12\bigr)\;=\;e^{\,2i\theta(q)}\qquad\text{(133)}
```

*and the point symmetry*

<a id="eq-yinf-pointsym"></a>

```math
Y_{\inf}\bigl(\tfrac12-x\bigr)\;=\;1-Y_{\inf}(x):\qquad\text{(134)}
```

*the map $`z\mapsto1-z`$, rotation by π about the point $`\tfrac12`$, carries the curve parametrized on $`[0,1]`$ onto the curve parametrized on $`[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]`$.*



*Proof.* For [(132)](#eq-psi-reflection), multiply through by $`\cos(2\pi x)`$ and write $`\varphi=2\pi x`$. On the left, $`\Psi(x)\cos(2\pi x)=\cos(A-\varphi)`$, since $`2\pi(x^2-x-\tfrac1{16})=A(x)-\varphi`$. On the right, $`\cos\bigl(2\pi(x+\tfrac12)\bigr)=-\cos(2\pi x)`$ and $`(x+\tfrac12)^2-(x+\tfrac12)=x^2-\tfrac14`$, so $`\Psi(x+\tfrac12)\cos(2\pi x)=-\cos\bigl(A(x)-\tfrac\pi2\bigr)=-\sin A`$; moreover $`\theta(x)=\varphi-A-\tfrac\pi2`$, so $`e^{i\theta}=-ie^{\,i(\varphi-A)}`$. The claim therefore reads

```math
\cos\varphi-\cos(A-\varphi)\,e^{-iA}
\;=\;(-\sin A)\,(-i)\,e^{\,i(\varphi-A)}
\;=\;i\sin A\,e^{\,i(\varphi-A)},
```

and expanding the left side settles it: the real part is $`\cos\varphi-\cos(A-\varphi)\cos A=\sin A\,\sin(A-\varphi)`$ (product formula), the imaginary part is $`\sin A\,\cos(A-\varphi)`$, and $`\sin A\bigl(\sin(A-\varphi)+i\cos(A-\varphi)\bigr)
=i\sin A\,e^{-i(A-\varphi)}`$, which is the right side.

For [(133)](#eq-yinf-halflag), two elementary phase relations do the work: $`A(q-\tfrac12)=A(q)-\varphi+\tfrac\pi2=-\theta(q)`$ and, applying [(132)](#eq-psi-reflection) at $`x=q-\tfrac12`$, $`Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)=\Psi(q)\,e^{\,i\theta(q-\frac12)}`$ with $`\theta(q-\tfrac12)\equiv2\varphi-A\pmod{2\pi}`$. Then

```math
Y_{\inf}(q)-Y_{\inf}\bigl(q-\tfrac12\bigr)
=1-\Psi(q)\bigl(e^{-iA}+e^{\,i(2\varphi-A)}\bigr)
=1-2\cos(A-\varphi)\,e^{-i(A-\varphi)}
=-e^{-2i(A-\varphi)}
=e^{\,2i\theta(q)},
```

using $`\Psi(q)\cdot2\cos\varphi=2\cos(A-\varphi)`$ in the middle step and $`\theta=\varphi-A-\tfrac\pi2`$ at the end.

For [(134)](#eq-yinf-pointsym), first $`\Psi(1-x)=\Psi(x)`$: the numerator of [(131)](#eq-psi-fn) is unchanged because $`(1-x)^2-(1-x)=x^2-x`$, and so is the denominator because $`\cos\bigl(2\pi(1-x)\bigr)=\cos(2\pi x)`$. Then, by the polar form [(132)](#eq-psi-reflection) evaluated at $`\tfrac12-x`$ together with $`\theta(\tfrac12-x)=2\pi(\tfrac12-x)(\tfrac12+x)-\tfrac{3\pi}8
=\tfrac\pi8-2\pi x^2=-A(x)`$,

```math
Y_{\inf}\bigl(\tfrac12-x\bigr)
=\Psi(1-x)\,e^{\,i\theta(\frac12-x)}
=\Psi(x)\,e^{-iA(x)}
=1-Y_{\inf}(x),
```

the last step being the definition [(130)](#eq-yinf). ◻


<a id="thm-yinf"></a>


**Theorem 11.3**. *Fix $`q\in(0,1)`$ and $`\sigma\in(0,1)`$. Then*

```math
\lim_{\substack{T\to\infty\\ \{T\}=q}} Y_{in1}(\sigma,T)\;=\;Y_{\inf}(q),
\qquad
\lim_{\substack{T\to\infty\\ \{T\}=q}} Y_{ang1}(\sigma,T)
\;=\;Y_{\inf}\bigl(q-\tfrac12\bigr),
```

*at rate $`O(1/T)`$, uniformly for q and σ in compact subsets of $`(0,1)`$; the limit does not depend on σ. On the critical line the proof is complete modulo Gabcke’s bound [(74)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-gabcke); off it, it additionally uses the first-order Siegel asymptotics [(64)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-R-general), with the same caveat as in Theorem [8.5](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#thm-d1-positive-offline).*



*Proof.* Throughout, *C₀* and Ψ are the same function, as [(136)](#eq-psi-is-c0) below records; we keep Gabcke’s symbol *C₀* for the analytic input and Ψ for the limit curve.

*Step 1 (the yin point in asymptotic closed form).* By [(108)](#eq-yin1), $`Y_{in1}=R\,(m+1)^{\sigma}e^{\,i\omega}`$. On the critical line $`R=e^{-i\vartheta}r`$ with *r* real, so $`Y_{in1}=\sqrt{m+1}\;r\,e^{\,iu}`$, and inserting [(73)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-r-two-cases)–[(74)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-gabcke),

<a id="eq-yin-asymp"></a>

```math
Y_{in1}
=\Bigl(\tfrac{m+1}{a}\Bigr)^{\!1/2}(-1)^{N-1}\bigl(C_0(\hat p)+E\bigr)
e^{\,iu}
\;+\;\bigl[N=m{+}1\bigr]\,\bigl(1+e^{\,2iu}\bigr),\qquad\text{(135)}
```

where the extra summand of [(73)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-r-two-cases) became the closed pair $`2\cos u\,e^{\,iu}=1+e^{\,2iu}`$. Off the line the first-order Siegel asymptotics [(64)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-R-general) give the same leading form: the frame factor $`(m+1)^{\sigma}e^{\,i\omega}`$ carries the two bracket pieces of [(64)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-R-general) to $`\bigl(\tfrac{m+1}{a}\bigr)^{\sigma}e^{\,i(\omega-\tilde\vartheta)}`$ and $`|\chi|\,a^{\sigma-1}(m+1)^{\sigma}e^{\,i(\omega+\psi+\tilde\vartheta)}`$, and since $`|\chi|=a^{1-2\sigma}\bigl(1+O(1/t)\bigr)`$ and $`\psi=-2\vartheta+O_\sigma(1/t)`$, both tend to $`e^{\,iu}`$; likewise the Iverson pair $`(m+1)^{-s}+\chi\,(m+1)^{s-1}`$ maps to $`1+|\chi|(m+1)^{2\sigma-1}e^{\,i(2\omega+\psi)}\to1+e^{\,2iu}`$. In every case

```math
Y_{in1}=(-1)^{N-1}C_0(\hat p)\,e^{\,iu}\bigl(1+O(1/T)\bigr)
+\bigl[N=m{+}1\bigr]\bigl(1+e^{\,2iu}\bigr)\bigl(1+O(1/T)\bigr).
```

*Step 2 (the phase, now with its full angle).* The expansion behind [(70)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-cosu), kept as an angle rather than used only through its cosine, reads

```math
u\;\equiv\;\pi(m+1)+\pi\bigl(\tfrac18-2\delta^2\bigr)+O(1/T)
\pmod{2\pi},
\qquad \delta:=a-(m+1)\longrightarrow q-\tfrac12,
```

(substitute $`t=2\pi a^2`$ and the asymptotic series of ϑ into $`u=t\log\tfrac{m+1}{a}+\tfrac t2+\tfrac\pi8+O(1/t)`$ and reduce $`\pi a^2`$ modulo $`2\pi`$), and $`\pi\bigl(\tfrac18-2(q-\tfrac12)^2\bigr)=\theta(q)`$, so $`u\equiv\pi(m+1)+\theta(q)+O(1/T)`$. Meanwhile $`\hat p\to q+\tfrac12`$ (with $`N=m`$) for $`q<\tfrac12`$ and $`\hat p\to q-\tfrac12`$ (with $`N=m+1`$) for $`q>\tfrac12`$, by [(69)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-cut-cases).

*Step 3 (case $`q<\tfrac12`$).* Here $`N=m`$, so $`(-1)^{N-1}e^{\,i\pi(m+1)}=(-1)^{2m}=1`$ and the Iverson term is absent:

```math
Y_{in1}\;\longrightarrow\;\Psi\bigl(q+\tfrac12\bigr)e^{\,i\theta(q)}
\;=\;Y_{\inf}(q)
```

by the reflection identity [(132)](#eq-psi-reflection).

*Step 4 (case $`q>\tfrac12`$).* Here $`N=m+1`$, the sign product is $`-1`$, and the Iverson pair is present:

```math
Y_{in1}\;\longrightarrow\;
1-\Psi\bigl(q-\tfrac12\bigr)e^{\,i\theta(q)}+e^{\,2i\theta(q)} .
```

Since $`\theta(q)=-A(q-\tfrac12)`$ exactly (Lemma [11.2](#lem-psi-reflection)), the first two terms are $`Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)`$, and the half-lag relation [(133)](#eq-yinf-halflag) finishes: $`Y_{in1}\to Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)+e^{\,2i\theta(q)}=Y_{\inf}(q)`$.

*Step 5 (the yang point).* By [(109)](#eq-yang1), $`Y_{ang1}-Y_{in1}=-\chi\,(m+1)^{2\sigma-1}e^{\,2i\omega}`$, whose modulus $`|\chi|(m+1)^{2\sigma-1}\to1`$ and phase $`2\omega+\psi=2u+O_\sigma(1/t)
\equiv2\theta(q)+O(1/T)`$, so the subtracted vector tends to $`e^{\,2i\theta(q)}`$ and $`Y_{ang1}\to Y_{\inf}(q)-e^{\,2i\theta(q)}=Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)`$, again by [(133)](#eq-yinf-halflag). One caution on reading this as a lag: by the point symmetry [(134)](#eq-yinf-pointsym), $`Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)=1-Y_{\inf}(1-q)`$, so within each unit interval the yang end runs the yin limit curve time-reversed and rotated by π about the point $`\tfrac12`$; equivalently, $`Y_{ang1}(\sigma,T)\approx1-Y_{in1}(\sigma,\,2m{+}1{-}T)`$ for every fractional part. The naive lag reading $`Y_{ang1}(\sigma,T)\approx Y_{in1}(\sigma,T-\tfrac12)`$ is correct only on $`\{T\}>\tfrac12`$, where *T* and $`T-\tfrac12`$ share the frame link $`m+1`$; for $`\{T\}<\tfrac12`$ the point $`Y_{in1}(\sigma,T-\tfrac12)`$ lives in the previous frame, and since $`Y_{\inf}`$ is not $`1`$-periodic, the two sides differ by order one there.

Every ingredient contributes $`O(1/T)`$: the amplitude ratio $`(m+1)/a=1+O(1/T)`$, Gabcke’s $`E=O(1/a)`$, the phase remainder in Step 2, and off the line the $`O_\sigma(1/t)`$ terms of ψ and $`|\chi|`$ together with the error of [(64)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-R-general) (relative $`O(1/T)`$, §[8.3](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-d1-approx-general)). At the seam $`q=\tfrac12`$ the two case formulas agree: $`\Psi(1)=\Psi(0)=\cos\tfrac\pi8`$ and $`1+e^{\,i\pi/4}=2\cos\tfrac\pi8\,e^{\,i\pi/8}`$, so both give $`\cos\tfrac\pi8\,e^{\,i\pi/8}`$, which is $`Y_{\inf}(\tfrac12)`$. ◻



**Remark 135** (numerical corroboration). The identities [(132)](#eq-psi-reflection)–[(134)](#eq-yinf-pointsym) check to thirty digits, and the convergence at the predicted rate is visible at moderate height: $`|Y_{in1}-Y_{\inf}(q)|`$ at $`q=0.2`$ is $`1.3\times10^{-3}`$ at $`T=40.2`$ and $`3.3\times10^{-4}`$ at $`T=160.2`$ for $`\sigma=\tfrac12`$, the same factor-of-four drop per quadrupling of *T* occurring at $`\sigma=0.3`$ and $`\sigma=0.9`$, with the yang errors matching the yin errors half a unit away in the argument. The Step-5 caution is equally visible: at $`T=80.2`$ the flipped relation $`|Y_{ang1}-(1-Y_{in1}(2m{+}1{-}T))|=4\times10^{-3}`$, while the naive lag $`|Y_{ang1}-Y_{in1}(T-\tfrac12)|=1.9`$, an order-one failure; at $`T=80.8`$, where the frames agree, the naive lag is fine ($`2\times10^{-4}`$). (`probe_yin_theorem.py`.)


<a id="cor-yinf-crossing"></a>


**Corollary 11.5** (the limit profile is the axis crossing of the limit chord). *For $`q\in(0,1)`$, $`q\neq\tfrac14,\tfrac34`$, the chord from $`Y_{\inf}(q)`$ to $`Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)`$ crosses the real axis at the abscissa $`d(q)`$, the limit profile of Theorem [8.8](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#thm-d1-limit).*



*Proof.* At every finite *T* the segment from $`Y_{in1}`$ to $`Y_{ang1}`$ is the reverse bisector link in the frame, and it crosses the real axis at the bisector point, at abscissa $`\hat d_1`$ (Figure [40](#fig-yinyang); §[11.2](#sec-derive-r1ps)). As $`T\to\infty`$ with $`\{T\}=q`$ the two endpoints converge to $`Y_{\inf}(q)`$ and $`Y_{\inf}(q-\tfrac12)`$ (Theorem [11.3](#thm-yinf)) while the crossing converges to $`d(q)`$ (Theorem [8.8](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#thm-d1-limit) on the line, its universality remark off it); away from $`q=\tfrac14,\tfrac34`$ the limit chord meets the axis transversally, so the crossing of the limit is the limit of the crossings. At $`q=\tfrac14,\tfrac34`$ the chord degenerates onto the axis itself (the parallel instants: it runs from $`\tfrac12`$ to $`-\tfrac12`$ and from $`\tfrac32`$ to $`\tfrac12`$ respectively), which is the limiting shadow of the off-line poles of §[8.4](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-pole-locations). The identity was also checked directly to thirty digits (`probe_yin_theorem.py`): the crossing of the limit chord and the closed form $`d(q)`$ of Theorem [8.8](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#thm-d1-limit) agree. ◻


So the two halves of the paper close into one loop: the weights $`d_1,d_2`$, their limit profile $`d(x)`$, and the yin-yang limit curve are a single asymptotic object seen from three angles, and the same amplitude runs through all of it.

Now the connection promised in the subsection title, which Step 1 of the proof has already put to work. The function [(131)](#eq-psi-fn) is, symbol for symbol, the universal amplitude

<a id="eq-psi-is-c0"></a>

```math
\Psi(x)\;=\;C_0(x)
\;=\;\frac{\cos\!\bigl(2\pi(x^2-x-\tfrac{1}{16})\bigr)}{\cos(2\pi x)}\qquad\text{(136)}
```

of the Riemann–Siegel first term used in §[8.2](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-d1-approx-critical)–[8.3](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-d1-approx-general) (equations [(63)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-d1-rs) and [(64)](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#eq-R-general)). So the same universal function plays both roles: evaluated at $`\hat p=\mathrm{frac}\sqrt{t/2\pi}`$ it is the asymptotic *magnitude* of the remainder, and evaluated at $`q=\mathrm{frac}(T)`$ it is the asymptotic *shape* of the yin-yang orbit; by [(130)](#eq-yinf) the limiting yin curve keeps distance $`|\Psi(q)| = |C_0(q)|`$ from the point $`1`$. Even the two parameters are the same clock read half a period apart: $`\sqrt{t/2\pi}\approx T+\tfrac12`$ (the Taylor table of §[10.1](10-i-t-functions.md#sec-IT-origin)), so $`\hat p\approx q\pm\tfrac12 \pmod 1`$, precisely the half-unit shift between the yang and yin arguments in Theorem [11.3](#thm-yinf).

Three properties of the limit curve deserve note. First, it is closed: $`Y_{\inf}(0)=Y_{\inf}(1)=1-\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{8})e^{\,i\pi/8}`$. Second, the apparent singularities of Ψ at $`q=\tfrac14,\tfrac34`$ (the pole heights of §[8.4](08-the-positive-real-function-d1-and-its-periodicit.md#sec-pole-locations)) are *removable* on the curve: $`\Psi\to\tfrac12`$ at both points, and the curve passes smoothly through the real values $`Y_{\inf}(\tfrac14)=\tfrac12`$ and $`Y_{\inf}(\tfrac34)=\tfrac32`$. Third, the reflection identity [(132)](#eq-psi-reflection) is a polar form of the curve, $`Y_{\inf}(q)=\Psi(q+\tfrac12)\,e^{\,i\theta(q)}`$: the same function that measures the distance $`|\Psi(q)|`$ from the point $`1`$ measures, half a period along, the distance $`|\Psi(q+\tfrac12)|`$ from the origin.
